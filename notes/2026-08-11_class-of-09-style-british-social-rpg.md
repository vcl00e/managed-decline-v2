# The legal question: you can absolutely touch politics, immigration and equality

You do **not** need to treat those subjects as forbidden.

Political expression is protected under Article 10 of the Human Rights Act framework, although that right is qualified rather than absolute. ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/schedule/1/part/I/chapter/9?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The important distinction is between **offensive/satirical material** and particular categories of unlawful material.

| What you do | Practical risk |
|---|---:|
| Mock fictional politicians and political movements | Low |
| Mock government policy or bureaucracy | Low |
| Characters argue about immigration | Low |
| Characters have racist/sexist/homophobic opinions as part of characterization | Context-dependent |
| Satirise DEI/equality bureaucracy | Generally low |
| Satirise anti-DEI activists | Generally low |
| Criticise religion | Generally permissible |
| Make jokes about British, French, Pakistani, Polish etc. cultural differences | Context-dependent |
| Falsely accuse a recognisable real person of corruption/crime/sexual misconduct | **High** |
| Publish material intended or likely to stir racial hatred | **High / criminal-law territory** |
| Threatening material intended to stir religious or sexual-orientation hatred | **High / criminal-law territory** |

### Defamation is probably your most mundane real-world hazard

In England and Wales, the Defamation Act 2013 requires serious harm to reputation, but putting obviously fictional names on characters doesn't necessarily make careless allegations about recognisable real people a good idea. The Act also contains defences such as truth, honest opinion and publication on a matter of public interest. ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/crossheading/requirement-of-serious-harm?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So this:

> The fictional Minister for Administrative Efficiency is unable to cancel his own £13.99 gym membership because he can't pass the government's digital identity check.

is much safer territory than:

> [Real living politician] takes bribes from [real company].

You can parody politicians. I'd simply avoid inventing defamatory *facts* about identifiable living people.

Also remember that defamation law is not identical across the UK: most of the Defamation Act 2013 extends only to England and Wales, while Scotland has its own Defamation and Malicious Publication (Scotland) Act 2021. A commercial UK-wide/international release deserves an actual media-law review near release. ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2013/26/pdfs/ukpga_20130026_en.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Immigration needs slightly more attention

The racial-hatred provisions are important because UK law's concept of a racial group includes **nationality, citizenship and national or ethnic origins**, and CPS guidance specifically notes its breadth. Racial hatred has a broader threshold than religious or sexual-orientation hatred: threatening, abusive or insulting material can qualify where the necessary intent/likelihood to stir racial hatred is present. ([cps.gov.uk](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/racist-and-religious-hate-crime-prosecution-guidance?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That does **not** mean:

> “You can't joke about immigration.”

Quite the opposite. You can have arguments about border policy, asylum hotels, labour migration, integration, multiculturalism, deportation policy, language, cultural conflict and so forth.

The safer and usually **funnier** target is:

**policy + bureaucracy + hypocrisy + individual behaviour**

rather than:

**ethnicity/nationality itself = reason this person is contemptible.**

For religion and sexual orientation, the specific stirring-up-hatred offences require threatening material and intent; CPS guidance expressly distinguishes hatred from mere offence, ridicule and jokes in the sexual-orientation context. ([cps.gov.uk](https://www.cps.gov.uk/publication/public-statement-prosecuting-racist-and-religious-hate-crime?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### The Equality Act isn't an “offensive comedy law”

The Equality Act regulates things such as discrimination and harassment in employment and provision of services. Section 29, for example, prohibits a service provider from discriminating against or harassing customers in specified ways. It isn't a general statutory prohibition against fictional characters saying politically incorrect things. ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/29?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So **mocking badly implemented equality initiatives is not automatically an Equality Act problem**.

# I would deliberately design for PEGI 16

This feels almost ideal for **PEGI 16** rather than trying to sanitize it to 12.

PEGI 16 accommodates stronger bad language, more realistic sexual activity and references to alcohol/tobacco/illegal drugs. Explicit sexual activity moves into PEGI 18 territory. PEGI's “Discrimination” descriptor applies to depictions of ethnic, religious, nationalistic or other stereotypes *likely to encourage hatred*, and that descriptor is always PEGI 18. ([pegi.info](https://pegi.info/))

So there's a useful creative constraint:

> **Make it outrageous enough to be 16 because the characters are awful—not 18 because the game itself appears hateful.**

That gives you a lot of room.

# Your best story engines

These would work especially well with the emergent system you've been describing:

1. **Housing engine.** Rents rise, boilers break, landlords conduct inspections, someone needs a guarantor, couples consider moving in together far too early, housemates disappear, councils intervene and someone inevitably begins illegally subletting a cupboard.

2. **Local-politics engine.** Elections, petitions, leaked WhatsApp messages, campaign leaflets, demonstrations, public consultations and opportunistic characters trying to turn whatever happened yesterday into their personal national political awakening.

3. **Migration/integration engine.** Do this through specific people and institutional consequences rather than “immigration debate simulator.” Contractors, employers, landlords, residents, migrants, lawyers, activists and politicians all want incompatible things.

4. **Public-services engine.** NHS appointments, police, social care, council repairs, benefits and transport become interconnected quests where helping one person can accidentally create problems for three others.

5. **Equality engine.** The joke isn't “minorities are silly.” The joke is that an **Inclusive Community Accessibility Festival** can plausibly be held upstairs in a building without a functioning lift because procurement already signed the contract.

6. **Cost-of-living engine.** Dating, food, heating, side jobs, splitting bills, stealing each other's Deliveroo vouchers and deciding whether falling in love is economically responsible.

7. **Rumour/outrage engine.** Something happens. Someone records 14 seconds of it. Context disappears. NPCs form opinions. A local politician comments. The player desperately attempts to explain what actually happened, thereby making everything worse.

That last system could become one of your game's signatures.

# The comedy formula

For this game, I'd use a repeating three-layer construction.

**Layer 1 — ridiculous line**

A character says something outrageous.

**Layer 2 — legitimate observation**

Buried underneath it is something uncomfortable but recognisable.

**Layer 3 — emotional vulnerability**

Why this particular person cares turns out to have almost nothing to do with the political argument they're having.

For example:

> **Council officer:** “We're not allowed to call it a housing crisis anymore.”  
> **Player:** “Why?”  
> **Council officer:** “Crisis implies something happened.”

That's political satire without requiring a political tribe to be the joke.

Or:

> **Activist:** “This meeting centres disabled lived experience.”  
> **Resident:** “Great. Why is it upstairs?”  
> **Activist:** “That's a facilities issue.”

The institution is being skewered, while the person supposedly benefiting gets to be the funniest person in the room.

That's the direction I'd pursue relentlessly.

## Practical legal/content rules for the writing room

Before release, I'd use a simple flagging system:

- **Real living person + negative factual allegation:** legal review.
- **Real company + allegation of illegality/dishonesty:** legal review.
- **Race/nationality/religion/sexual orientation + threatening/hate-promoting material:** legal review or rewrite.
- **Slur:** ask what the scene gains from it and whether the context clearly matters.
- **Protected characteristic itself is the entire joke:** usually rewrite—not merely because of law, but because it's probably weak satire.
- **All romanceable/sexual characters:** explicitly adults.
- **Real political policies:** jokes are fine; distinguish factual claims from absurd inventions.
- **Class of '09 references:** use it as a tonal reference internally, not a source to copy characters/dialogue/UI from.
- **Final flagged script + trailer/store copy:** pay a UK media/defamation solicitor for a pre-publication review.

One other consequence of your earlier NPC design matters here. Ofcom says the Online Safety Act can apply to online games that allow users to generate/share content that other users encounter, while publisher-produced offline/original game content is treated differently. So **single-player authored/controlled NPC interaction is legally much simpler than turning this into an unrestricted multiplayer social platform**. ([ofcom.org.uk](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/the-online-safety-act-and-gaming-know-the-risks-know-the-rules-know-how-to-comply))

Given how provocative this game's dialogue should be, that's another argument for the constrained dialogue-generation architecture we discussed rather than giving an unrestricted LLM permission to improvise political/racial/sexual jokes.

## Assistant

Yes. **Current TTS is technically good enough to voice a commercial game convincingly**, but I would not use it uniformly.

For your particular project, the best result is probably **human-directed synthetic VO + selective human performance**, rather than either “everything live-generated” or “no voices at all.”

## Why TTS can work now

Current high-end speech systems support emotional delivery, pacing controls, pronunciation dictionaries, multi-speaker dialogue and very low-latency streaming. ElevenLabs, for example, documents emotional control and dialogue-oriented generation; it also explicitly notes that generation remains non-deterministic, meaning identical settings do not guarantee identical performances. ([elevenlabs.io](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/speech-synthesis/voice-settings?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That last point matters.

The problem isn't:

> “Will it sound like a robot?”

Increasingly, no.

The problem is:

> **“Will it deliver this particular joke exactly correctly?”**

For your game, that's much harder.

A line like:

> “No, that's fine. I'm sure the landlord *needs* a third Range Rover.”

can be destroyed by putting the emphasis on the wrong word.

Comedy depends on:
- pauses
- interruptions
- contempt
- hesitation
- breath
- escalation
- deliberately flat delivery
- characters talking over each other
- suddenly dropping the joke and becoming sincere

A good human actor will infer those things from context. TTS sometimes nails them and sometimes gives you a perfectly natural **wrong performance**.

So you need to treat TTS generation like recording actors:

**generate takes → direct → audition → regenerate → edit → approve.**

Not:

**script → API → ship.**

# I think the ideal solution is this

| Dialogue type | Voice treatment |
|---|---|
| Major comedic set pieces | **Fully voiced, curated** |
| Romance/confession/breakup scenes | **Fully voiced, heavily directed** |
| Main story scenes | Mostly fully voiced |
| Important character banter | Voiced |
| Procedural/emergent conversations | Text + partial vocalisation |

| Generic reactions | Reusable recorded/synthetic reactions |
| Player dialogue choices | Probably silent |

That gives you most of the perceptual benefit for a fraction of the production burden.

> “Jesus Christ.”

Then display the generated text.

Then the text.

Or a laugh, sigh, inhale, irritated click, etc.

Now **you still hear Erin's personality every few exchanges**, without needing flawless runtime TTS for everything.

I think that would work extremely well with your NPC architecture.

# For authored dialogue, synthetic voices could be very good

Here I would go much further.

Generate the **entire conversation together**, not each line blindly.

Modern systems increasingly use surrounding dialogue and textual cues to influence prosody, and current dialogue-generation systems can account for multiple speakers, interruptions and emotional context. ([elevenlabs.io](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/playground/text-to-speech?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Your internal script format could contain direction:

```text id="hg8f7i"
ERIN
state: furious_but_hiding_it
pace: fast
subtext: actually hurt
beat_before: 250ms

"Yeah. No. Obviously that's completely fine."
```

And then:

```text id="eioicx"
TAKE 1
TAKE 2
TAKE 3
TAKE 4
```

Your narrative designer picks one.

For really important scenes, manually edit pauses or splice/re-render individual phrases.

At that point **TTS becomes an animation/rendering tool rather than an autonomous actor.**

That's a much safer production philosophy.

# Runtime TTS is where I'd be conservative

Technically possible?

**Absolutely.**

Desirable for every line?

**I don't think so.**

Besides performance inconsistency, shipping live-generated AI introduces another distribution consideration. Steam currently distinguishes **pre-generated** AI content from **live-generated** AI content; live generation requires developers to describe the guardrails they use against illegal output. ([partner.steamgames.com](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey?l=german&language=english&utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Your constrained dialogue architecture helps considerably.

But it creates a strange situation:

Your carefully written funniest scene is competing against a machine improvising voice performances in real time.

There's little reason to accept that risk.

Use runtime TTS where **breadth is valuable**.

Use pre-rendered performance where **quality is valuable**.

# What if you skip VO completely?

The game can still be good.

But **your specific game would lose something meaningful.**

Your premise relies heavily on:

> personality → conversational collision → outrageous escalation → emotional reversal.

Voice is exceptionally useful there.

Without VO, you'd need to compensate through:

- extremely strong portrait animation
- expressive character sprites
- excellent typography
- text timing
- sound effects
- reaction animations
- character-specific text presentation
- aggressive editing
- significantly stronger BGM

And you'd probably want less dialogue.

A lengthy unvoiced conversation feels longer than exactly the same conversation being performed extremely well.

### Roughly:

| Approach | Fit for your project |
|---|---:|
| Completely unvoiced | **6/10** |
| Live TTS everything | **6–7/10** |
| Pre-generated TTS everything | **8/10** |
| Traditional human VO everything | **9/10 quality, terrible scalability** |
| **Curated synthetic + human direction + partial systemic VO** | **9/10 overall fit** |

Those aren't objective quality scores—they're how I'd rank the approaches against **your design constraints**.

## What “illustrated 3D diorama” actually means

The environment is genuinely **3D**, but the player experiences it almost like 2D.

Imagine a little model town viewed from above at perhaps a 35–45° angle. Buildings have depth, buses can drive behind things, characters walk around each other, doors open, signs stick out from shops—but the camera is largely fixed.

That means **no square JRPG tiles are visible**. You aren't moving one tile north/south/east/west. You simply push the stick or WASD and walk naturally along the pavement.

It also avoids a lot of conventional 3D-game complexity. There is no jumping, climbing, aiming, camera rotation, elaborate collision or precision movement. You're basically moving a character around a small stage.

A useful technical description would be:

> **3D environment + orthographic camera + almost-2D controls.**

An orthographic camera also gives objects that pleasing miniature/dollhouse appearance instead of realistic perspective.

## The default gameplay loop could be extremely simple

I would aim for something like this:

1. **Walk around for 30–90 seconds.** You see characters, small incidents and visual jokes developing naturally.
2. **Notice something interesting.** An argument outside the pub, someone waiting at the bus stop, a phone notification, a crowd outside the council.
3. **Choose whether to intervene.** Walk over and press one interaction button.
4. **The camera effectively says “this matters now.”** The game transitions into a polished illustrated dialogue scene with portraits, VO and music.
5. **Make a few meaningful decisions.** Usually conversation, occasionally a simple contextual interaction.
6. **Return to the little town.** Something has changed: someone leaves, another person turns up, you get a message, time advances, perhaps another opportunity has disappeared.

That can basically be the entire game.

You don't need to constantly invent minigames just to justify calling it an RPG.

---

# Think **sitcom**, not open world

This is probably the most useful mental model.

A sitcom might repeatedly use:

**the flat → the pub → workplace → street**

and remain interesting because **different people and situations occupy those spaces**.

Your game can do exactly the same thing.

The pub doesn't need 15 rooms. It might be one beautifully composed little building that you can enter.

The council doesn't need eight realistic floors. Perhaps you only ever use:

**reception + office + meeting room.**

The player's flat might have:

**living room + kitchen + bedroom.**

You are building **sets**, not a simulation of every square metre of Britain.

That keeps it light.

---

# Visually, I'd make everything slightly toy-like

Not hyper-realistic 3D people.

Characters during exploration could be deliberately simplified:

chunky proportions, simple faces, clear silhouettes, slightly exaggerated walks and poses.

Then their **proper illustrated portrait** appears during dialogue.

This solves a major production problem.

You don't need expensive facial animation in the 3D world because the player isn't expected to stare at the little 3D Erin during a five-minute emotional conversation.

The exploratory model only needs to communicate:

> That's Erin.  
> She's angry.  
> She's walking toward the council building.

Then conversation mode gives you the expensive acting.

# The British identity can make the diorama charming

This format gives you lots of low-cost environmental comedy.

The player walks past:

**TO LET**

**PHONE REPAIR / VAPES / LUGGAGE**

**Chicken Cottage–type takeaway**

an estate agent inexplicably occupying the nicest unit on the high street

a permanently closed public toilet

three different charity shops

bus shelter advert

pub garden with two people stubbornly sitting outside in bad weather

little council planters

badly parked delivery van

someone dragging a wheely bin

a train crossing in the background

These become tiny visual punchlines rather than assets requiring interaction.

The town itself develops personality.

# You could make it even simpler than free movement

There are actually two viable versions.

### Version A — free walking

Probably my preference.

The player freely walks around each small diorama. It's tactile and makes NPC encounters feel spontaneous.

But locations remain **tiny**. A high street might take 20 seconds to walk end-to-end.

### Version B — point-and-click diorama

Even simpler.

The player sees the miniature environment and clicks:

**Erin**

**Pub**

**Council**

**Bus stop**

The character automatically walks there.

This removes almost all navigation friction while retaining the feeling that everyone occupies a shared physical world.

For a dialogue-heavy game, this is surprisingly attractive.

I might actually support **both**:

> Left stick/WASD = walk  
> Click/tap destination = auto-walk

---

# I'd avoid a huge continuous town

This is an important correction to the earlier idea.

You don't really need:

> one enormous seamless borough.

Instead use perhaps **6–10 small dioramas** connected through very short transitions.

For example:

**High Street**

**Council Square**

**Housing Estate**

**Station**

**Pub interior**

**Player flat**

**Clinic**

**Community Centre**

You select a distant destination from your phone or walk to a bus stop.

Then:

> brief transition → next little model set.

Much cheaper.

Much more readable.

And every location can have its own musical identity.

---

# Conversations should visually contrast with exploration

I would make the distinction obvious.

### Exploration

Small characters.  
Lots of environment.  
Ambient sound.  
Light BGM.  
Short speech bubbles occasionally.

Then you trigger an important interaction.

### Conversation

Environment softens/dims.

Large illustrated character appears.

Music changes.

Voice acting becomes prominent.

The entire screen becomes about **faces and timing**.

That's where you get the *Class of '09*-type strength without making the whole game a VN.

Then the scene ends and:

> *pop*

you're back inside your tiny high street.

That rhythm should feel extremely pleasant.

---

# Don't over-systematise the diorama

Because you want light-hearted simplicity, I wouldn't make every NPC a fully simulated *Sims* character.

You don't need to model:

> hunger = 72  
> bladder = 31  
> commute route optimization  
> realistic traffic

You mostly need:

> Erin is currently at pub.  
> At 18:00 she'll go to protest.  
> If argument happened, she'll instead go home.  
> If player invited her, she goes with player.

That's enough to create the illusion that people have lives.

The same applies visually.

You don't need 100 pedestrians.

Maybe **8 people visible on the high street**, with a bus occasionally going past.

It will feel more intentional and more like an illustrated comedy than a cheap attempt at an open-world simulation.

---

# If I had to lock the visual direction today

I'd choose:

**Low-detail 3D diorama environments**  
+ **fixed orthographic camera**  
+ **stylised simplified 3D characters**  
+ **very short walking distances**  
+ **hand-drawn 2D portraits for conversations**  
+ **speech bubbles for incidental dialogue**  
+ **VN presentation for substantial scenes**  
+ **phone UI for navigation/tasks/messages**.

That is probably the cleanest compromise between **RPG presence** and **VN production efficiency**.

And crucially, it shouldn't *look* like someone took a Japanese RPG template and changed the signs to English.

It can instead look like someone built a **tiny, affectionate, slightly ridiculous model of modern Britain and filled it with troublesome people**.
