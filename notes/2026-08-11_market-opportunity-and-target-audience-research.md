## Bottom line

**There is a real opportunity for *Managed Decline*. I would pursue it.** The market is not empty, but the exact combination we have been discussing is surprisingly under-served:

> **A funny, character-first game about ordinary life in contemporary Britain, where society is fraying around you, you can form relationships and meddle with local situations, and your actions cause stories to evolve.**

That is importantly different from **“a game about British politics.”**

As of **8 August 2026**, British election/political simulators are becoming a noticeable little niche, but polished **British social comedy built around characters and relationships** remains comparatively unusual. # 1. The strongest evidence: Britishness itself can sell internationally

The most encouraging comparator is actually **Thank Goodness You're Here!**

It is unapologetically Northern English rather than generically international: Barnsworth, regional voices, British food, bleakness, local eccentrics, working-class/post-industrial imagery and extremely specific British humour. It currently has around **8,300 Steam reviews across all review types**, with 96% positive English-language reviews. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2366980/Thank_Goodness_Youre_Here/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That matters because it disproves one concern I had:

> **You do not need to sand off the Britishness to make the game exportable.**

Its specificity is part of the product.

Reviews and press repeatedly discussed precisely that local character. The Guardian described its Northern English eccentricity and dark humour, while other coverage explicitly discussed its post-industrial setting and social undercurrent. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/games/article/2024/jul/31/thank-goodness-youre-here-review?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So I would actively lean into:

- NHS waiting rooms.
- letting agents.
- councils.
- Jobcentres.
- trains.
- high streets.
- pubs.
- local Facebook-group mentality.
- private contractors.
- universities.
- landlords.
- supermarkets.
- schools.
- neighbourhood politics.
- British class anxiety.
- polite euphemisms for things obviously going catastrophically wrong.

Not references for their own sake, but **institutions embedded into people's lives**.

That gives *Managed Decline* an identity instantly.

# 2. Class of '09 is an unusually relevant comparator

*Class of '09* now has roughly **9,000 total Steam reviews**, while *The Re-Up* has about **4,700**, with extremely high positive ratings. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1443200/Class_of_09/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The resemblance isn't the visual novel format. It is the proposition:

**specific place + specific generation + outrageous characters + recognisable social reality + comedy + branching situations.**

The creator explicitly described *Class of '09* as being culturally rooted in the Washington DC/Beltway area rather than using the location merely as scenery. ([butwhytho.net](https://butwhytho.net/2024/04/interview-class-of-09-creator-max-field-sbn3/))

That is exactly the direction I'd take here.

*Managed Decline* should not be:

> generic anime characters who happen to say “mate” occasionally.

It should feel as though these people **could only have grown out of Britain in this particular era**.

That is much more defensible creatively.

# 3. Political/social satire has proven demand too

There are several useful adjacent examples.

**Not For Broadcast** is probably the closest successful political-comedy comparison. It combines British political satire, dark comedy, choices and narrative systems and currently has more than **5,000 English Steam reviews at 94% positive**. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1147550/__Not_For_Broadcast/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Not Tonight** went even closer to contemporary Britain: an alternate post-Brexit UK, RPG/simulation mechanics, immigration bureaucracy and political satire. It has roughly **2,800 reviews across review types**, with more than 2,200 purchaser reviews. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/733790/Not_Tonight/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

And something particularly interesting happened in Poland. **Politics The Game**, released in October 2025, is a comedic adventure/political simulation explicitly inspired by Polish politics. It has already accumulated roughly **2,466 reviews at 94% positive**. That is good evidence that culturally specific political comedy does not have to appeal only to its home country's audience. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2999060/Politics_The_Game/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So there is demonstrated demand for all three pieces:

**social comedy → yes**  
**British comedy → yes**  
**political satire → yes**

The interesting part is the intersection.

# 4. But “UK political simulator” is becoming crowded

This is where I would **not** position the game.

There is now **Governance**, a current British political simulation where you can be an MP, party leader or Prime Minister. It presently has only **32 Steam reviews**, despite directly targeting contemporary British politics. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4489310/Governance/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is also an upcoming **UK Politics Simulator**, along with *War for Westminster* and other titles focused on elections, parties and policy. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4119600/UK_Politics_Simulator/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

And **Keeping Up**, listed in 2026, describes itself almost exactly as a “satirical British life sim,” centred on jobs, studying, money and an ominous weekly algorithm. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4816030?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There are smaller titles such as *A Very British Summer* too. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3942150/A_Very_British_Summer/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### This creates a useful distinction.

The crowded-ish space is:

> **run Britain / win an election / become Prime Minister / manage an economy**

The much emptier space is:

> **live in Britain and survive everybody else trying to run it.**

That second one is where *Managed Decline* belongs.

# 6. So I would change how we describe the game

I would **not** lead with:

> Political satire JRPG/VN about modern Britain.

That sounds niche, argumentative and text-heavy.

The **politics becomes the source of circumstances**, rather than the primary genre.

That makes the potential audience dramatically broader.

# 7. The smartest thing about this idea may be its marketing potential

This game could be an unusually effective **clip generator**.

And there is a remarkably strong case study for that.

The creator of *Class of '09* described its commercial history in detail. The game originally released in 2021 and remained fairly obscure. Then on **24 June 2023**, a clip of its “mall cop” scene went viral. He says the game immediately started selling much better. Later that year an animated short exploded online and, in his words, increased momentum by roughly **10–20×**. ([butwhytho.net](https://butwhytho.net/2024/04/interview-class-of-09-creator-max-field-sbn3/))

Coverage of the series likewise notes that gameplay/anime clips spreading through TikTok and other short-form platforms were central to its breakout. ([theliteraryplatform.com](https://theliteraryplatform.com/stories/all-writing-needs-to-be-looked-into/))

That is almost tailor-made for *Managed Decline*.

## Every story engine should be capable of producing 20–60 second comedy clips.

For example:

**Council encounter**

> PLAYER: The bin hasn't been collected in three weeks.  
> COUNCIL OFFICER: That's encouraging.  
> PLAYER: How?  
> COUNCIL OFFICER: Last month it was four.

Then escalation.

Or an absurd train interaction.

Or Tabitha explaining some catastrophically euphemistic policy.

Or two NPCs arguing.

Or a romance scene being interrupted by some bureaucratic nightmare.

Or someone discovering that the local library has become a “community-facing book-adjacent flexible asset.”

These clips don't require someone to understand the game first.

**They are entertainment on their own.**

That is extremely valuable.

# 10. Steam should be the conversion endpoint

Put the **Coming Soon page up much earlier than release**, once the visual identity and screenshots genuinely look good.

Valve explicitly recommends publishing a Coming Soon page once you have appropriate branding/screenshots/description, because it gives people somewhere to wishlist the game and activates its Community Hub. Wishlisters are automatically notified at launch. ([partner.steamgames.com](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/coming_soon?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So every piece of external content should ultimately point toward:

**Wishlist Managed Decline on Steam.**

Not:

follow our Twitter  
join our mailing list  
subscribe to our YouTube  
follow our Discord  
read the devlog  
then perhaps wishlist.

Too much friction.

Steam is the endpoint.

# 11. But don't obsess over wishlists as an algorithm hack

Valve explicitly says wishlists are **mostly not a direct factor in algorithmic visibility**, although they matter for things such as Popular Upcoming and automatic notifications. Tags and actual customer interest are important to Steam's recommendations. ([partner.steamgames.com](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/visibility?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So the goal isn't:

> somehow manufacture 10,000 wishlists.

It is:

> create 10,000 people who genuinely want this game.

There is a difference.

That also means buying questionable ads simply because they produce cheap wishlists can give you misleading signals.

# 12. A demo should be one of our primary marketing weapons

This project's structure lends itself unusually well to a demo.

It could simply be:

**one small neighbourhood + 8–12 NPCs + several interconnected situations + one substantial escalation + enough simulation for people to discover things independently.**

Not a tutorial.

Not Chapter 1 of a thirty-hour plot.

A **little playable episode of Managed Decline**.

That makes YouTubers' jobs easy.

Steam's official Next Fest documentation explicitly describes the festival as a way of building an audience and collecting feedback around playable demos. ([partner.steamgames.com](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Recent data collected from the February 2026 event suggests middling participating games can add a few thousand wishlists, while charting games can add around 15,000 and exceptional breakouts substantially more. Those figures are third-party benchmarks rather than Valve guarantees. ([howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/04/13/making-sense-of-the-february-2026-steam-next-fest/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Importantly, recent analysis found **pre-Fest momentum strongly correlated with performance during Next Fest**. In other words, Next Fest works better as an amplifier than a resurrection machine. ([howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/03/26/benchmarks-how-many-wishlists-can-i-get-from-steam-next-fest/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So:

**don't rush into Next Fest.**

Use the **last appropriate Next Fest before release**, after the game already looks compelling.

# 17. There is one marketing trap I would explicitly avoid: “AI game”

Even though AI-assisted production and intelligent systems may be important **under the bonnet**, I would not lead with:

> REVOLUTIONARY AI NPCs!

Sell:

> **Every character has their own relationships, priorities and memories. Things happen even when you're not there.**

The latter describes a player benefit.

There is also a current commercial reason for this. A 2025 Quantic Foundry survey of 1,799 respondents found **85% expressed below-neutral attitudes toward generative AI in games**, with negative sentiment particularly high around artwork, music and narrative/dialogue; story-motivated players were also comparatively more negative. ([quanticfoundry.com](https://quanticfoundry.com/2025/12/18/gen-ai/))

Meanwhile Steam currently requires developers to disclose AI-generated player-facing content in its Content Survey, with additional guardrail disclosures where content is generated live while the game is running. ([partner.steamgames.com](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey))

So AI should be a **production/system technology**, not the brand promise.

The brand promise is:

**“These people feel alive.”**

- camera;
- exploration;
- map presentation;

# My market verdict

I would **not** abandon this idea because the market is crowded.

I'd do almost the opposite.

The research suggests that we have accidentally landed near the intersection of several proven markets without sitting directly inside any of them:

**Class of '09**  
→ outrageous character comedy + social accuracy + branching narrative. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1443200/Class_of_09/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Thank Goodness You're Here!**  
→ culturally specific British comedy + beautiful distinctive presentation + ordinary people. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2366980/Thank_Goodness_Youre_Here/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Not For Broadcast / Not Tonight**  
→ British social/political satire players will actually pay for. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1147550/__Not_For_Broadcast/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Modern narrative-game market**  
→ demonstrably capable of producing substantial indie hits despite ferocious overall Steam competition. ([howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/01/27/what-the-hell-happened-in-2025/))

The mistake would be making **“a political game complaining about Britain.”**

The opportunity is making **a genuinely entertaining social RPG/comedy about people living in a recognisable, ridiculous Britain**, where there is affection underneath the satire and the player can become part of the mess.

And because comedy scenes, character interactions and emergent disasters are inherently shareable, **this is one of the rare game concepts where I think a small team can plausibly substitute creative output for a large advertising budget.**

The next prototype I would build after the movement/camera work is therefore **not another technical prototype**. It would be the **“viral vertical slice”**: a 15–25 minute playable chunk of *Managed Decline* specifically designed to test character appeal, British identity, emergent storytelling, comedy timing **and whether it naturally generates clips people want to share**. That would tell us far more about the commercial viability of the project than another several months of systems development.

That would materially misidentify the market.

The highest-probability paying audience is:

> **18–40-year-old PC/Steam players who become attached to characters, enjoy dark or absurd comedy, want meaningful agency in stories, and are comfortable with games where talking, exploring and social consequences replace conventional combat.**

Within that, there are **three genuinely different core customers**. A fourth audience can become substantial if we design the relationship systems correctly. Political-game enthusiasts are a fifth, smaller audience.

There is no reliable dataset that can tell us the exact demographic distribution of an unreleased hybrid game, so I will distinguish **observed evidence** from my **planning inference** rather than pretending we know percentages.

## The audience map

| Audience | Likely age band* | What they already buy | Why they would buy *Managed Decline* | Importance |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| **Character/fandom players** | 18–29 | *Class of '09*, *Slay the Princess*, *Danganronpa*, *Life is Strange*, *Night in the Woods* | Characters, chemistry, romance, drama, quotable comedy, identity | **Core** |
| **Narrative-RPG players** | 23–40 | *Disco Elysium*, *Esoteric Ebb*, *Citizen Sleeper*, *Scarlet Hollow*, *Pentiment* | Agency, consequences, role-playing, systemic NPCs, exploration | **Core** |
| **Quirky/British comedy players** | 22–45 | *Thank Goodness You're Here!*, *Untitled Goose Game*, *Stanley Parable* | Humour, Britishness, art direction, absurd situations | **Core** |
| **Relationship/life-sim players** | 18–35 | *Fields of Mistria*, *Stardew Valley*, *Coffee Talk*, relationship-heavy RPGs | Belonging, romance, friendships, town life, character routines | **Expansion audience with major upside** |
| **Political/social-satire players** | 25–45 | *Not For Broadcast*, *Suzerain*, *Papers, Please*, political CRPGs | Institutions, politics, social commentary, moral choices | **Secondary** |
| **Clip spectators** | heavily <30 | Watch narrative/comedy games rather than necessarily buying | Funny characters and outrageous scenes | **Marketing audience, not necessarily customers** |

\*The age bands are **my launch-planning inference**, not measured demographics for these games.

The critical discovery is that the first four groups overlap substantially. **That overlap is the opportunity.**

# 1. Customer #1: the character/fandom player

This may eventually be the most powerful audience even if it isn't initially the largest source of sales.

Think of someone around 18–29 who doesn't primarily ask:

> “What are the mechanics?”

They ask:

> “Who are these people?”

They form favourite characters. They discuss relationships. They quote lines. They make edits. They draw fan art. They ship characters. They argue about whether a character is secretly awful or merely traumatised. They replay a scene to see a different reaction.

They might play visual novels, but **they do not necessarily identify as a visual-novel enthusiast**.

That distinction is visible in *Class of '09*. Its own Steam pitch calls it the visual novel for people who hate visual novels. Its tags cluster around comedy, choices, female protagonist, satire, multiple endings, dark comedy, story-rich and nonlinear play. It currently has about **8,420 Steam-purchaser reviews**, 94% positive among English reviewers. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1443200/Class_of_09/))

More importantly, we have unusually good evidence about how its audience materialised. Creator Max Field says the sequel was selling poorly until a specific “mall cop” scene went viral on **24 June 2023**. Sales immediately improved. An animated short later exploded online and he says momentum increased by roughly **10–20×**. The interviewer describes the breakout audience as anime fans and people interested in irreverent humour. ([butwhytho.net](https://butwhytho.net/2024/04/interview-class-of-09-creator-max-field-sbn3/))

There is another interesting signal. The creator was principally writing from a millennial recollection of late-2000s American high school, yet self-selected discussions inside the *Class of '09* fandom repeatedly describe a surprisingly young, female and queer audience. That Reddit evidence is anecdotal and **cannot establish demographics**, but it strongly suggests that characters can attract an audience quite different from the one a creator originally imagines. ([reddit.com](https://www.reddit.com/r/Classof09Game/comments/1fz16tq/is_the_class_of_09_fandom_filled_with_teens/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### What this person wants from *Managed Decline*

They don't buy because Tabitha is an incisive parody of a particular government communications archetype.

They buy because:

**Tabitha is Tabitha.**

They want to see her:

meeting someone she hates;  
getting drunk;  
having a disastrous date;  
trying to defend an indefensible policy;  
getting unexpectedly upset about something;  
reacting differently because of something the player did six hours ago.

This player wants characters with enough psychological coherence to become **people in their head**.

That is why the stylised face-to-face VN interactions we have been working toward matter commercially, not merely aesthetically.

# 2. This audience will probably contain a lot more women and queer players than a conventional indie RPG

I would explicitly design for that possibility.

Large Quantic Foundry datasets find that women, on average, over-index on **Story, Fantasy/role-identification, Design/expression and Completion**, while men over-index on Competition, Challenge and several action-oriented motivations. Their genre analysis found enormous variance *within* RPGs: Western RPGs in their sample averaged 26% female, but *Dragon Age: Inquisition* reached **48%**, demonstrating that genre labels don't predetermine audience composition. Atmospheric exploration and family/farm simulation also had the highest non-binary participation in their genre analysis. ([quanticfoundry.com](https://quanticfoundry.com/2015/08/28/gender-differences-in-gaming/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That research is older, so I wouldn't use those percentages to forecast 2026 sales. The underlying point is much more useful:

> **Design determines who shows up.**

A combat-heavy CRPG, a romance-heavy RPG and a political strategy game may all say “RPG” on Steam and acquire completely different audiences.

Quantic Foundry's newer 2025 report is based on more than **466,000 gamers**, specifically segments ages 15–64 by age and gender for game positioning. Their broader 2026 dataset now contains almost **2 million respondents**, although it is explicitly skewed toward core PC/console gamers. ([quanticfoundry.com](https://quanticfoundry.com/insight-report/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is also a meaningful LGBTQ audience. GLAAD/Nielsen research found **17% of active US PC/console gamers identify as LGBTQ**, and 63% of LGBTQ gamers said the option to play as lesbian, gay or bisexual would make them more likely to buy or play a game. Those numbers are US-specific rather than global. ([glaad.org](https://glaad.org/glaad-gaming/2024/1-in-5-active-gamers-are-lgbtq/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So inclusive romances and player identity aren't merely “representation features.” For this particular type of game they can intersect directly with **purchase motivation and fandom formation**.

Not by ticking boxes. By writing very good characters.

---

# 3. Customer #2: the narrative-RPG obsessive

This customer is different.

Probably somewhat older on average, more Steam-native, and considerably more suspicious of superficiality.

They've played some combination of:

*Disco Elysium*, *Citizen Sleeper*, *Pentiment*, *Roadwarden*, *Scarlet Hollow*, *Baldur's Gate 3*, *Suzerain* and now *Esoteric Ebb*.

They care about the sentence:

> **“The game actually remembers what I did.”**

Much more than:

> “15 romanceable characters!”

This person examines systems.

They will deliberately antagonise someone just to see whether the game handles it.

They will follow an NPC home.

They will discover that two characters meet without them.

They will reload and try the situation from another angle.

They will talk online about an outcome another player didn't even know existed.

### There is extremely current evidence for this buyer.

*Esoteric Ebb* was released only on **3 March 2026**. It explicitly sells itself as a funny “Disco-like” CRPG involving an election, political conspiracy, bizarre characters, branching dialogue, failure states and choices that return to bite you.

As of today it already has roughly **7,995 Steam-purchaser reviews**, with 95% positive English reviews, despite a €24.99 price point. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

That is remarkably close to one dimension of what we're proposing.

And older comparators remain huge. *Night in the Woods* has over **13,000 English Steam reviews** around exploration, story, characters, dark humour and a deteriorating town. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/481510/night_in_the_woods/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So the person interested in:

> dysfunctional people + crumbling community + humour + socioeconomic reality

very clearly exists.

---

# 4. But this customer will punish us if *Managed Decline* is secretly just a VN

This is probably the greatest product-design risk.

If we tell the narrative-RPG player:

> “Intelligent NPCs! Emergent social world! Your actions matter!”

and what they actually get is:

walk to NPC → trigger dialogue → choose A/B/C → relationship +1,

they will see through it immediately.

For this buyer, the **walking around matters because the world must be inspectable and manipulable**.

Scarlet Hollow is instructive. It advertises not merely dialogue but dynamic relationships, 21 character builds, radically branching content, and relationships ranging from rivals and enemies to friends and acquaintances. It claims a single playthrough exposes less than a fifth of the content, and currently has about **4,672 Steam-purchaser reviews** with overwhelming positive sentiment. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1609230/Scarlet_Hollow/))

That buyer wants the *Managed Decline* promise to be:

> “I had a story happen because of what I did.”

not:

> “I watched the story the writers selected.”

That is precisely where our emergent-character architecture could become commercially differentiating.

---

# 5. Customer #3: the person who just thinks British absurdity is funny

This group is easy to underestimate because some of them are **not enthusiasts for VNs or CRPGs at all**.

They bought *Thank Goodness You're Here!*.

Steam itself classifies that audience through tags such as:

Comedy, Funny, Hand-drawn, Exploration, Casual, Short and Cartoon.

It currently has **7,598 purchaser reviews**, 96% positive English reviews, and Steam bundles it with *Untitled Goose Game*, *Time Flies*, *to a T* and *Later Alligator*. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2366980/Thank_Goodness_Youre_Here/))

That's an identifiable consumer taste:

> **weird, distinctive, authored indie things that make me laugh.**

They don't require fifty hours of progression.

They may own a Steam Deck.

They might normally play one odd indie every few months.

They discover something because somebody sends them a trailer saying:

> “This looks extremely British.”

### This person's media taste matters as much as their game taste.

*Thank Goodness You're Here!* deliberately draws on extremely British comedy traditions. Its creators have discussed influences such as Reeves and Mortimer and *The Mighty Boosh*; contemporary criticism similarly connected it with Northern working-class comedy and surreal British traditions. ([thegamer.com](https://www.thegamer.com/thank-goodness-youre-here-interview-report-coal-supper-the-good-time-garden-2-james-carbutt-will-todd-british/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For *Managed Decline*, I would expect affinities around things like *Peep Show*, *The Thick of It*, *Alan Partridge*, *Brass Eye*, *Taskmaster*, *The Office*, British panel-show humour and contemporary internet satire.

That's an **inference about likely media affinity**, rather than measured purchaser data, but it gives us an important creative test:

> Someone should be capable of finding *Managed Decline* hilarious even if they have never touched a JRPG.

---

# 6. And they do not need to be British

This surprised me somewhat.

Of *Thank Goodness You're Here!*'s 7,598 Steam-purchaser reviews, 5,820 are English-language, but there are also substantial Simplified Chinese, Russian and Brazilian-Portuguese review populations. The game supports eleven interface/subtitle languages. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2366980/Thank_Goodness_Youre_Here/))

*Not For Broadcast* provides an even more dramatic example. It is extremely British and extremely political, yet only **5,279 of its 10,255 Steam-purchaser reviews are English-language**. Steam shows another 2,115 Simplified-Chinese and 1,709 Russian reviews; the game has full voice localisation for Chinese and Russian. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1147550/Not_For_Broadcast/))

That changes my view of the addressable audience.

### *Managed Decline* should be authentically British, not Britain-only.

My probable launch priority would be:

**UK + US + Canada + Ireland + Australia/NZ first**, then localisation markets according to wishlist/demo evidence.

Don't flatten the jokes for America.

Explain enough context through the situation that the joke survives without the player having read BBC News that morning.

# 7. Customer #4 could become enormous: the relationship/life-sim player

This is the audience I would be most interested in experimentally pulling toward the project.

Look at *Fields of Mistria*.

Its proposition combines town restoration, character customisation, routine, exploration, friendship and romance. As of today it has about **27,587 Steam-purchaser reviews** and 22,004 English-language reviews. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2142790/Fields_of_Mistria/))

Or *Coffee Talk*: a game fundamentally about sitting down and listening to people's personal problems. It has approximately **4,704 English Steam reviews**, with tags including Dialogue Heavy, LGBTQ+, Story Rich, Emotional and Choices Matter. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/914800/Coffee_Talk/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This customer is not necessarily looking for political satire.

They want:

> **a place I can inhabit and people I can know.**

That is tremendously compatible with our diorama-town concept.

The difference is tone.

If *Managed Decline* is relentlessly cynical and everyone is awful, this group will leave.

If beneath the comedy there are:

friendships,  
people genuinely caring for one another,  
romances,  
community solidarity,  
small victories,  
familiar places,  
recurring routines,  
people trying to make things better,

then this audience could become substantial.

And that actually supports the thematic intention we've been discussing: **“come on, we can do better” rather than “Britain is terrible and everyone is an idiot.”**

---

# 8. Customer #5: politically engaged people

They are real.

They are just **not the customer I would build the product around**.

*Not For Broadcast* proves that political satire plus meaningful interaction can sell: it currently has more than **10,000 Steam-purchaser reviews** and markets 14 endings, consequential decisions, dark comedy and both national and family-level consequences. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1147550/Not_For_Broadcast/))

*Esoteric Ebb* provides the current RPG-side validation. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This person might listen to political podcasts, follow elections and recognise references others miss.

But there's a dangerous difference:

The character player asks:

> “What is Tabitha going to do?”

The political hobbyist asks:

> “Which real politician is Tabitha supposed to represent and does the developer agree with me?”

The first question builds a fandom.

The second can turn your community into Culture War Customer Support.

### Let this audience find the game.

Don't let them define it.

# 9. The biggest false audience: teenagers watching clips

This deserves particular attention because it can completely corrupt our market signals.

A *Managed Decline* clip could get millions of views among people who **never buy the game**.

*Class of '09* is instructive again. Its creator explicitly describes TikTok virality driving sales, so short-form reach absolutely matters. But he also says an animated short received **1.5 million YouTube views without recouping its production cost**. Games themselves were what people paid for. ([butwhytho.net](https://butwhytho.net/2024/04/interview-class-of-09-creator-max-field-sbn3/))

And the *Class of '09* community contains a visibly younger fandom than the original late-2000s nostalgic premise would suggest, according to self-reports and community discussions. Again, that's anecdotal rather than representative demographic research. ([reddit.com](https://www.reddit.com/r/Classof09Game/comments/1fz16tq/is_the_class_of_09_fandom_filled_with_teens/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For us:

**teenage viewers are useful reach, not the commercial target.**

Especially if the finished game's humour and themes justify a mature age rating.

# 10. The crucial difference between our viewer and our buyer

Imagine we publish this exchange:

> Council worker: “The waiting list has actually fallen by 40%.”  
>   
> Player: “You closed the waiting list.”  
>   
> Council worker: “Which is why there are fewer people on it.”

That can produce a viewer.

### To create the buyer, they then need to learn:

**I could have sided with her.**

**I can work for that department.**

**She remembers that I embarrassed her.**

**Her colleague now dislikes me.**

**Someone told my romantic interest what I did.**

**The council's decision changed something elsewhere in town.**

**My friend's playthrough went completely differently.**

That's the monetisation boundary.

**Comedy gets attention. Agency sells the game.**

# 11. The audience is therefore much less “VN” than I initially thought

This is a meaningful conclusion from the research.

Traditional VN customers will certainly exist.

But if we market exclusively as a VN, we voluntarily shrink the addressable audience.

Steam itself shows successful games blurring these categories. *Esoteric Ebb* carries tags for **CRPG, RPG, Interactive Fiction, Narrative and Visual Novel** simultaneously. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

*Class of '09* deliberately rejects conventional VN expectations while being one. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1443200/Class_of_09/))

*Scarlet Hollow* explicitly bridges adventure, VN, RPG-like traits and relationship simulation. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1609230/Scarlet_Hollow/))

I therefore think the public-facing genre should eventually be something closer to:

> **A character-driven social RPG set in a tiny contemporary British town.**

Then explain that conversations, friendships, romance, jobs, reputation and meddling replace conventional combat.

“VN/JRPG hybrid” is useful terminology **for us while designing it**, not necessarily the thing customers should see first.

# 12. Gender: don't pick a male target

If I were writing the audience brief for the team today, I would write:

> **Design for a mixed-gender audience. Assume women and queer players may comprise an unusually important part of the fandom. Do not code the presentation as a male-targeted RPG with women as rewards.**

That's not an ideological recommendation. It's a commercial one supported by the game we're designing and the motivation evidence.

Quantic Foundry specifically warns that even within a genre, individual titles can differ enormously in gender composition, with presentation, playable identity, relationships and other design dimensions potentially producing much larger variation than genre averages. ([quanticfoundry.com](https://quanticfoundry.com/2017/01/19/female-gamers-by-genre/))

And their separate work found that preference for third-person presentation correlates with stronger **Story** motivation, including interest in narrative, NPCs and NPC relationships—another small data point in favour of the more embodied, character-visible presentation we've been developing rather than a purely first-person conversation simulator. ([quanticfoundry.com](https://quanticfoundry.com/2023/01/27/perspective/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

# 13. Age: I think there are really two centres of gravity

Not “the average gamer is 25.”

More like:

### **18–27: fandom centre**

Character-first.  
More likely to encounter clips first.  
High potential for fan art, ships, edits, memes and community activity.  
Potentially stronger attraction to romance and identity expression.

### **27–40: purchasing/review centre**

More likely to already understand *Disco Elysium*, *Citizen Sleeper*, political satire and British institutional humour.

More willing to spend £20-ish on an unusual single-player game **if the reviews say the writing and choices are exceptional**.

There will obviously be substantial crossover.

I wouldn't narrow advertising targeting to these bands as though we had hard demographic evidence. They are **product personas**, not census categories.

And the broader market doesn't support the old idea that games are primarily for teenagers anyway: ESA's 2026 US research reports weekly gaming across 71% of Millennials and 56% of Gen X, while Ofcom reports 89% of UK 16–24s game. ([theesa.com](https://www.theesa.com/two-thirds-of-americans-play-video-games-every-week-according-tonew-report-from-the-entertainment-software-association/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

# 14. What I think the *ideal Managed Decline customer* actually looks like

Not by superficial demographics. By behaviour.

They're someone who might say:

> “I loved *Disco Elysium*, but sometimes I just wanted to spend more time living around the characters.”

or:

> “I don't normally play VNs, but *Class of '09* was hilarious.”

or:

> “I bought *Thank Goodness You're Here!* because it looked completely unlike everything else.”

or:

> “I spent an embarrassing amount of time getting to know everyone in *Fields of Mistria*.”

or:

> “I replayed *Scarlet Hollow* because I wanted to know whether that character could end up hating me instead.”

**The overlap between those statements is our customer.**

That is much more useful than:

> Male, 18–35, likes RPGs.

# 15. The product decisions will actually choose our audience

This is probably the most important result of the research.

| If we emphasise… | Audience we increasingly acquire |
|---|---|
| Politics, elections, policy | Political-sim crowd; more niche |
| Stats, builds, skill checks | CRPG/*Disco* crowd |
| Beautiful town + absurd jokes | Broad quirky-indie crowd |
| Romance + character intimacy | Fandom/relationship audience |
| Player customisation | Role-identification/life-sim audience |
| Dynamic NPC lives | Social-RPG / simulation audience |
| Anime-looking portraits | Younger VN/anime fandom |
| Distinct British illustration | Broader comedy/art-game identity |
| Constant bleak cynicism | Edgy-comedy/politics audience |
| Cynicism **plus genuine human warmth** | Much broader character/life-sim audience |
| Scripted branches only | VN/adventure audience |
| Emergent consequences | RPG/simulation audience |

**I would optimise for the intersection in bold:**

> ### character fandom × narrative RPG × British comedy × relationship simulation

and allow political satire to live **inside that product**.

That is a much more attractive market than “political visual novel.”

# 16. What this changes about the actual game

The research makes me more confident about some parts of the prototype direction and less confident about others.

The town should be **beautiful enough that people want to inhabit it**.

The VN confrontation view should make characters **attractive, expressive and memorable enough to form attachments to**.

NPC simulation should generate **social consequences the player can actually observe**.

Romance should be significant, but friendships, enemies, colleagues, neighbours and family relationships need similar depth.

The player needs enough identity/expression that **my protagonist** feels distinct from another player's.

Comedy should arise primarily from **characters and circumstances**, not streams of references to whichever government minister happens to be in the news when we write it.

And underneath the satire there needs to be **something worth saving**.

Those aren't merely artistic preferences now. They map directly onto the four strongest potential audiences the market evidence points toward. ([store.steampowered.com](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2366980/Thank_Goodness_Youre_Here/))

## My current customer definition

If I had to give a publisher the target audience in one paragraph today:

> **Managed Decline targets young-adult and adult players who enjoy character-driven, story-rich indie games, particularly fans of reactive RPGs, dark comedy, relationship games and distinctive authored worlds. Its primary audience overlaps players of Class of '09, Disco Elysium/Esoteric Ebb, Scarlet Hollow/Night in the Woods and Thank Goodness You're Here!, with a significant potential expansion into life-sim and romance audiences. The game should expect a mixed-gender audience with meaningful female and LGBTQ participation, and should treat politically engaged players as a secondary constituency rather than its defining demographic. Its initial commercial market is likely Steam-first and Anglosphere-heavy, with substantial international potential through localisation.**

And I think there's a very practical next research step: **build a proper competitor/audience matrix of perhaps 20–30 adjacent games and extract their Steam tags, review counts, price, localisation, estimated ownership, follower trajectory, creator coverage, subreddit/fandom behaviour and positioning.** That would let us turn these qualitative audience cohorts into a much more defensible **market-size and sales forecast for Managed Decline**, and tell us which exact audience is commercially large enough to design the vertical slice around.
