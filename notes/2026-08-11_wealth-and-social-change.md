## Assistant

There is **a lot in this**, and I think it could make *Managed Decline* substantially more interesting—provided wealth is treated as a **change of social world**, not as the player “winning” and escaping the premise.

In fact, the contrast could strengthen the central idea: Britain is not one experience. The same country can feel broken, beautiful, generous, suffocating, absurd, cultured, hostile, comfortable and precarious depending on where you are standing.

## The key distinction: wealth should open another Britain

I would avoid:

> Poor Britain → player succeeds → Rich Britain → everything is lovely now.

That would flatten the satire and accidentally imply that the solution to social decline is simply becoming rich enough not to notice it.

Instead:

> **Britain A → something changes your social position → Britain B becomes visible → everything you previously understood gets reinterpreted.**

That is much stronger.

Suddenly the player starts encountering people who complain about entirely different things.

Someone in the opening hours is panicking because their landlord is raising the rent £120.

Later somebody is furious because the council rejected the planning application for their orangery.

And the important thing is that **both characters genuinely believe they are having a terrible week**.

That is very fertile territory for the tone you've been developing.

---

# It also solves your “nice Britain” problem

You absolutely should show lovely Britain.

But I wouldn't make all the lovely bits wealth-gated.

The early game should already contain moments like:

old stone villages in evening light, countryside footpaths, an unexpectedly beautiful local churchyard, a nice old pub, a summer fête, museums, Victorian parks, independent cafés, coastal walks, ridiculous little local traditions, community events, old railway architecture, gardens, canals, university towns, autumn streets, Sunday roasts, people actually being decent to one another.

Otherwise the game's thesis becomes **“Britain is horrible”**, which is much less interesting than what I think you're actually reaching for:

**“This place is wonderful. Why on earth are we letting parts of it become like this?”**

That underlying affection is what gives the criticism weight.

Then wealth introduces *another category* of beautiful Britain: immaculate Georgian streets, country houses, beautiful schools, private gardens, expensive restaurants inside absurdly old buildings, sailing clubs, gallery openings, quiet affluent villages, elegant London neighbourhoods, country weekends, cultural institutions, old family homes, surprisingly eccentric wealthy people, etc.

And importantly, those places shouldn't secretly all be awful either.

Some of them should be **genuinely brilliant**.

The player should occasionally think:

> Oh. I get why people want this.

That makes the class commentary considerably sharper.

---

# The wealth transition could be one of the game's major structural turns

I wouldn't make this happen at the beginning.

Imagine roughly:

| Phase | Player experience |
|---|---|
| **Early game** | Local life, school/uni/work, friends, dating, housing, transport, mundane British institutional nonsense |
| **Middle game** | Player becomes deeply embedded in a particular social network and understands everyone's relationships |
| **Disruption** | Some absurd sequence gives the player substantial money/status/access |
| **Second world** | New locations, social circles and opportunities become available |
| **Collision** | Old and new social worlds begin interacting |
| **Late game** | Player decides what kind of person they're actually becoming |

That fourth and fifth phase are where the idea becomes particularly good.

Because the most interesting content isn't actually **meeting rich people**.

It's taking the relationships you've already established and changing one variable:

**you now have money.**

---

# Every existing relationship changes slightly

This is exactly the kind of thing your intelligent-character / emergent relationship system can exploit.

Your best mate might be delighted.

Another friend might become quietly resentful.

Someone who previously rejected the protagonist might suddenly become interested—and the player won't know whether the money has anything to do with it.

A politically committed friend might start treating the protagonist as a class traitor.

Someone struggling financially may begin asking for increasingly uncomfortable amounts of help.

Another person will conspicuously refuse every offer because they don't want to become dependent on you.

Your parents' behaviour might change.

Romantic relationships become especially interesting.

Imagine dating someone *before* the money appears versus meeting exactly the same person *afterward*.

Those relationships contain completely different uncertainties.

The person who knew you beforehand becomes valuable partly because:

**they knew you when you were nobody.**

That's emotionally powerful territory, not just satire.

---

# And the wealthy characters shouldn't simply be villains

This part matters enormously.

Some should be insufferable.

Some should be astonishingly nice.

Some should be socially oblivious but fundamentally decent.

Some should have inherited money and feel deeply embarrassed by it.

Some should genuinely believe enormous privilege is normal because they've never experienced anything else.

Some should be newly wealthy and considerably more status-obsessed than the aristocratic characters.

Some old-money eccentric might own a decaying manor, drive a filthy 2003 Volvo and have £140 in their current account.

Someone else might be leveraged to the eyeballs while projecting enormous wealth.

A genuinely wealthy character might be the **least materialistic person in the cast**.

That's recognisably British and much more interesting than *Rich People Bad*.

It also gives you another comic vocabulary.

Early-game characters obsess over Universal Credit forms.

Later-game characters casually say things like:

> “Daddy had to sell the smaller place in Provence.”

And everyone in their social circle reacts as though this is a serious family tragedy.

---

# I would not make the protagonist secretly rich from the beginning without care

The “parents were rich all along” version can work, but it has a specific danger.

If the protagonist spent the whole opening living under genuine financial constraints and then discovers:

**actually Mum and Dad had £8 million**, 

the player may reasonably ask why any of the previous hardship happened.

It can retroactively undermine the first half of the story.

There are versions where it works, though.

Perhaps a parent is estranged from an extremely wealthy family.

Perhaps there is a ridiculous inheritance nobody expected.

Perhaps some incredibly complicated family dispute finally resolves.

Perhaps a grandparent deliberately excluded one generation.

That makes the wealth **newly accessible**, rather than revealing the protagonist's earlier circumstances were fake.

But I actually like an accidental windfall more for this game.

Especially because it could be ludicrously British.

Not necessarily a lottery win. Something bureaucratic and convoluted would feel much more *Managed Decline*.

For example, after an absurd 12-event chain involving a dead relative, a council compulsory purchase, a forgotten parcel of land, a solicitor who has been writing to the wrong address for eleven years, and an inexplicable property valuation…

the protagonist receives £1.7 million.

And everyone just goes:

**“…what?”**

That fits your tone.

---

# Don't make them *too* rich

This is another important calibration.

£500 million fundamentally changes the genre.

The protagonist stops participating in society and starts controlling parts of it.

But something like **£1–5 million of unexpected wealth**, or a very successful career/business route, creates much more interesting ambiguity.

They're rich enough to:

buy property,
travel,
enter expensive social spaces,
stop worrying about ordinary expenses,
help friends,
meet genuinely wealthy people,
be treated differently.

But they are **not remotely in the same category** as someone whose family owns half of Wiltshire.

So the player discovers that “rich” itself has layers.

They get £2 million and think:

> I'm rich.

Then they enter a world where someone's parents own a £35 million estate.

And that person feels poor next to someone whose family has £800 million.

That could be a fantastic running joke.

The social ladder keeps extending upward.

---

# Mechanically, money should change the map

This is where it fits the JRPG/VN hybrid particularly well.

The map doesn't merely unlock **expensive shops**.

Entire **social ecosystems** unlock.

The protagonist receives invitations they never received before. Characters who previously ignored them recognise them. New locations become available. Certain conversations become easier while others become harder. NPC assumptions about the protagonist change.

Your simulation can effectively have an invisible variable something like:

**Perceived Class Position**

which isn't identical to actual money.

It could be affected by wealth, accent, clothes, occupation, education, neighbourhood, friends, reputation and behaviour.

That gives you very British situations where somebody has money but doesn't know the codes.

The protagonist turns up wearing what they think is an extremely expensive outfit.

Everyone else is wearing battered jumpers.

They have accidentally identified themselves as **new money** within twelve seconds.

That is excellent interactive comedy.

---

# You also get a very good second progression system: learning the codes

Instead of an RPG where you level up Strength and Magic, the protagonist gradually learns how different bits of society work.

Initially they don't understand why people say:

> “That's quite brave.”

when they actually mean:

> “That is a fucking terrible idea.”

They don't know which schools somebody means when they say “school.”

They don't understand why nobody is talking about money despite everyone obviously having enormous amounts of it.

They don't realise that the person wearing gardening clothes owns the building.

You can have the player slowly acquire **social literacy** without explicitly turning it into a visible “Upper Class +3” stat.

And characters should notice mistakes.

---

# The strongest part is when the two worlds collide

Imagine that late in the game the player throws a birthday party.

Their original friends come.

Their new friends come.

Several romanceable characters come.

Parents come.

Nobody quite knows how to talk to anyone else.

**That party could generate dozens of storylines depending on everything the player has previously done.**

Or the protagonist invites their old mate to some absurdly luxurious weekend.

Maybe the friend loves it.

Maybe they hate being patronised.

Maybe they embarrass the protagonist.

Maybe the protagonist is embarrassed that they're embarrassed.

Maybe the rich characters actually adore them.

Maybe the old friend starts dating someone from the new circle.

Suddenly your social simulation becomes extremely interconnected.

That is precisely where your emergent design has an advantage over a conventional VN.

---

# It gives the game a better overarching question

Without this element, *Managed Decline* risks becoming predominantly:

> Look at all these ridiculous things happening in modern Britain.

With it, the game can gradually become about:

> **What actually makes a good life here?**

Money?

Community?

Status?

Security?

Love?

Beautiful surroundings?

Belonging?

Freedom?

Being useful?

Having people who knew you before you became somebody?

And different characters can give radically different answers without the game announcing which one is correct.

Someone living in a fairly miserable flat might have an incredibly strong community.

Someone living in an £8 million house might be desperately lonely.

And then, importantly, **don't make that simplistic either**.

Someone in an £8 million house can also have a wonderful family, meaningful work, great friends and an extremely nice life.

Sometimes privilege really does make things easier.

The game doesn't have to manufacture cosmic punishment to make inequality morally legible.

That restraint would make the writing much smarter.

---

## There is one structural choice I would make

I would **not guarantee the wealth arc in every playthrough**.

Make it one of the game's major possible trajectories.

Perhaps the player can end up:

ordinary but deeply embedded in their community;

professionally successful;

accidentally wealthy;

politically involved;

artistically successful;

socially disastrous;

married into another social world;

or some weird combination.

But the **wealth route should be unusually substantial**, almost like discovering a second half of the game hidden inside the first.

Players will talk about it:

> “Wait, you've never done the inheritance chain?”

> “The WHAT?”

That is exactly the kind of discovery that suits the game you're designing.

And because the world simulation continues rather than restarting, the player gets to experience the genuinely fascinating part:

**the same Britain, the same friends, the same protagonist — but from a completely different position inside it.**

I think that strengthens *Managed Decline* rather than diluting it. It turns “decline” from the aesthetic of the game into one perspective among several, while letting the game show what is beautiful, ridiculous and worth preserving about Britain in the first place.

## I think the structure should look more like this

### 1. The original world has to become substantial first

Before wealth enters the picture, the player needs enough accumulated social history that changing their circumstances actually means something.

They should already have:

- several close relationships
- grudges and awkward history
- favourite places
- routines
- financial constraints
- people they rely on
- people who rely on them
- romantic possibilities
- an emerging sense of identity
- opinions about the people and institutions around them

Otherwise becoming wealthy just unlocks a different map.

If all of that exists first, wealth becomes a **perturbation of an existing simulation**.

That distinction is fundamental.

---

# 2. Foreshadow the other Britain from the beginning

The wealthy world should not suddenly materialise.

The player should occasionally brush against it long before they can participate in it.

Perhaps:

- a friend's family is surprisingly wealthy
- somebody works catering at expensive events
- the player walks through affluent areas
- a romanceable character went to a completely different kind of school
- they briefly enter an expensive house
- someone has an absurdly wealthy relative
- they encounter politicians, professionals, landowners, academics or arts-world people through ordinary circumstances
- an apparently ordinary NPC turns out to have a very privileged background

This establishes:

> **This world has always existed five miles away from you. You just weren't part of it.**

Then accessing it later feels like a revelation rather than a sequel.

---

# 3. Don't have the money instantly change their life

This is probably the most important change.

Suppose the player discovers they are going to receive £2 million.

For quite a while, they should still be living exactly where they were.

There might be:

solicitors, forms, probate, tax questions, arguments, family members, waiting periods, decisions, suspicion, people finding out, people *not* finding out.

That creates a fascinating transitional state:

> **Technically rich. Socially exactly the same person.**

Then the player gets to decide what starts changing.

Do they quit their job?

Tell everyone?

Buy a house?

Pay off somebody else's debt?

Keep pretending nothing happened?

Buy one ludicrous thing?

Move away?

That makes the transition playable.

---

# The liminal period may actually be the best part

For several hours the protagonist should belong completely to neither world.

They have money but don't know wealthy people.

They can afford places but don't understand them.

Old friends don't quite know whether anything has changed.

The protagonist doesn't know whether anything has changed either.

That is much more interesting than:

**Achievement unlocked: Upper Middle Class**

It also gives the story time to metabolise what happened.

---

# 4. Let the player move upward in gradients

I wouldn't immediately introduce country estates and aristocrats.

Think of it as concentric social layers.

### Layer 0 — Existing life

Their original social circle.

### Layer 1 — Comfortable life

Nice flat/house, good restaurants, holidays, no immediate financial anxiety.

This alone should feel transformative.

### Layer 2 — Affluent professional Britain

Lawyers, doctors, architects, finance people, successful creatives, academics, entrepreneurs.

Now the protagonist encounters people for whom £100,000 salaries aren't remarkable.

### Layer 3 — Serious wealth

Private clubs, major property owners, established families, expensive schools, collectors, political donors, senior institutions.

The protagonist realises:

> “Oh. I'm not rich here.”

### Layer 4 — The genuinely absurd end

Maybe only occasionally encountered.

Generational estates, enormous inherited wealth, people whose families have known each other for centuries.

These characters shouldn't become the protagonist's everyday peer group unless the story takes a very particular route.

That progression prevents tonal whiplash.

---

# 5. Keep returning to the same places

This would do an enormous amount of work.

Imagine visiting the same local high street:

**early game:** counting money before buying lunch.

**later:** casually eating somewhere nicer.

**much later:** owning a property nearby or trying to help somebody whose shop is closing.

Same geography.

Different relationship to it.

Or the same pub.

Initially it's where everyone meets because it's cheap.

Later the protagonist returns after spending weeks circulating in much more affluent environments.

Nothing about the pub has changed.

But the protagonist's perception of it has.

That's far more emotionally powerful than simply unlocking **Rich Town**.

---

# 6. Don't replace the cast

This is where it would become Part Two.

If the wealth event causes:

> New map  
> New cast  
> New houses  
> New problems  
> New romances  
> New gameplay

then yes, you've essentially made another game.

Instead, the new social world should **attach itself to the existing graph**.

Maybe 60–70% of the people who matter deeply after the transition should still be people we've already met.

New characters expand the graph.

They don't replace it.

So instead of:

```text id="fcimo5"
OLD WORLD

A — B — C — D

============

NEW WORLD

W — X — Y — Z
```

you want:

```text id="kewbcv"
        W —— X
       /      \
A —— B —— PLAYER —— Y
 \       /        /
  C —— D ————— Z
```

The interesting stories occur across those connections.

---

# 7. Money should introduce problems without doing the cliché "money can't buy happiness"

Because, frankly:

**money solves a lot of problems.**

The game should acknowledge that.

Housing anxiety disappears.

Transport becomes easier.

Unexpected bills stop being frightening.

The protagonist gets more choices.

They can help people.

They get time.

They gain access to beautiful things.

That's real.

The dramatic consequences come from **the second-order effects**, not punishment for being wealthy.

For example:

> You can now afford to move.

But should you?

> You can give your friend £20,000.

But what happens afterward?

> You don't need your terrible job anymore.

But half your social circle came from work.

> You can solve your parents' mortgage.

But will they accept it?

> You're now dating someone considerably poorer than you.

Who pays for everything?

There doesn't need to be a moral answer.

---

# There should also be characters who barely care

This is important for realism.

Somebody you've known for years should react to the protagonist becoming wealthy with approximately:

> “Oh, nice. Anyway, are you still coming Tuesday?”

Those people become surprisingly important emotionally.

They stabilise the continuity of the game.

---

# I would also reconsider making the wealth event purely accidental

It could be accidental.

But I think we should test several forms because they tell slightly different stories.

| Mechanism | What it explores |
|---|---|
| Inheritance | class, family, generational wealth |
| Property/legal accident | absurd institutions, luck |
| Business success | merit, luck, capitalism, ambition |
| Career success | status, work, social mobility |
| Partner/family connection | dependence, class crossing |
| Lottery/windfall | pure randomness |
| Combination | probably most natural for emergent play |

I'm increasingly attracted to **multiple ways of entering the wealthy trajectory**.

Not necessarily equivalent amounts of money.

One protagonist might become successful professionally.

Another inherits property.

Another marries someone wealthy.

Another experiences a bizarre windfall.

And perhaps these routes converge into some shared high-level systems while retaining different social consequences.

That is much more compatible with the emergent game we're designing.

---

# There is an even more interesting possibility

**The wealthy world doesn't need a single unlock event at all.**

The player could slowly cross into it.

Maybe:

1. they become friends with someone affluent;
2. get invited somewhere;
3. meet another person there;
4. start earning more;
5. change job;
6. date somebody from that world;
7. unexpectedly inherit £400k;
8. buy somewhere;
9. five years of simulated social mobility have effectively occurred through gameplay.

At some point the player notices:

> Wait. When did my character become rich?

That might actually be stronger.

Then the spectacular inheritance/windfall can still exist as one rare route where the transition happens much faster.

---

# We should prototype the **transition**, not the wealthy world

Before we design twenty wealthy characters and ten affluent environments, I think we should answer one question:

> **Does changing class position make the existing game more interesting?**

We can test that very cheaply.

Take perhaps **6 existing characters and 3 locations**.

Build a hypothetical mini-arc:

### State A

Player has £2,500 savings and ordinary income.

We establish their relationships.

### Inciting event

Player learns they will receive a substantial sum.

### State B

Nobody knows.

Run some conversations.

### State C

Two friends know.

Run the same social system.

### State D

Money becomes available.

Allow maybe four meaningful choices:

- do nothing
- quit job
- buy property
- give someone money

### State E

Introduce just **one** affluent location and **two** affluent characters.

Then return immediately to the original cast.

If that produces interesting consequences, we have validated the concept.

If instead everyone feels like they're waiting around for *Rich Britain DLC* to begin, we know the structure is wrong before committing enormous scope to it.

---

## I would judge that prototype against five questions

**1. Does the protagonist still feel like the same person?**

If no, transition too abrupt.

**2. Do old relationships become more interesting?**

If no, wealth is functioning as content replacement rather than a systemic modifier.

**3. Does the original map remain relevant?**

If no, we're accidentally building Part Two.

**4. Does the player feel simultaneously excited and socially destabilised?**

That's probably the emotional sweet spot.

**5. Does the new Britain make the old Britain more interesting in retrospect?**

This is the big one.

If experiencing privilege makes the player reinterpret earlier events, characters and places, then the mechanic is doing real thematic work.

---

## And I think there should be a hard scope rule

For every **one new wealthy-world system**, we should try to get **two or three new interactions with existing content**.

For example:

Adding property ownership shouldn't merely create a housing interface.

It should alter:

- conversations with the player's parents,
- conversations with a renting friend,
- where parties happen,
- romance logistics,
- neighbourhood relationships,
- financial requests,
- perhaps political conversations.

That is how this becomes **depth rather than scope creep**.

---

The concept I would currently put at the centre of the design is therefore not:

> **Halfway through the game, the protagonist gets rich.**

It is:

> **The game allows the protagonist's position within British society to change dramatically, while the world and people around them persist.**

That formulation is much safer structurally. Wealth becomes the most dramatic example of a broader system—**social mobility and changing belonging**—rather than a giant second campaign bolted onto *Managed Decline*.

And I think that broader system is potentially one of the game's defining features.

The core question is not really **“how does the player get rich?”** It is:

> **What does mastery of Managed Decline look like?**

And I don't think the answer should be accumulating money, XP, relationship points, or finding the correct dialogue route. It should be **becoming increasingly capable of shaping your life and the lives around you**.

## Progress should mean increasing agency

A strong progression model could have several dimensions operating simultaneously:

| Dimension | Early-game experience | Progress feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial security** | Bills constrain choices | More freedom, savings, better housing, eventually wealth |
| **Relationships** | Acquaintances and fragile friendships | Trust, intimacy, history, dependable people |
| **Social network** | You know whoever happens to be nearby | You know who to call when something happens |
| **Competence** | Limited abilities and confidence | You can actually solve increasingly difficult situations |
| **Knowledge** | You don't understand people/institutions | You understand how things really work |
| **Reputation** | Nobody particularly knows you | Different communities have opinions about you |
| **Access** | Most of Britain is just scenery | Homes, workplaces, organisations and social circles open up |
| **Influence** | Things happen to you | You can increasingly make things happen |
| **Home/lifestyle** | Makeshift and constrained | A life that increasingly reflects your choices |
| **Identity** | Mostly undefined | The accumulated consequences create a recognisable version of the protagonist |

Crucially, **these shouldn't collapse into one score**.

A player can be wealthy and lonely.

Or broke but enormously influential locally.

Or professionally successful but hated.

Or have a modest life, brilliant relationship, lovely home and strong community.

Or become socially connected enough that they seem to know someone everywhere.

Those are different successful character builds.

That immediately prevents wealth from becoming the obvious victory condition.

# The main reward should actually be **options**

This could be one of the defining design principles.

In a conventional RPG:

> Kill enemy → get XP → increase stat → kill bigger enemy.

In *Managed Decline*:

> Understand situation → make clever decision → change relationships/world state → **new possibilities become available.**

Suppose someone's landlord is trying to throw them out.

Early game, perhaps you can sympathise or help them move.

Later, depending on how you've lived, you might know a solicitor, know somebody at the council, have enough money to help, know a journalist, know the landlord, understand housing law, have a spare room, know someone with a vacant flat, or convince several people to work together.

That is progression.

The **same type of problem becomes richer because your accumulated life gives you more ways to interact with it.**

That seems much more appropriate for this game than Strength 17 → Strength 18.

---

# And this gives us a strong definition of player skill

I think *Managed Decline* should reward three overlapping kinds of mastery.

**Understanding people** means remembering personalities, relationships, insecurities, loyalties and social context. **Understanding systems** means figuring out workplaces, housing, institutions, class codes, money, bureaucracy and how different communities function. **Creative composition** means seeing that three apparently unrelated resources can be combined into a solution nobody explicitly offered you.

That third one is particularly important.

The game shouldn't constantly present:

> A) Good solution  
> B) Neutral solution  
> C) Stupid solution

Instead, the player learns:

> I know Priya works in planning.  
> Darren knows the owner.  
> Tabitha wants publicity.  
> I've got £700 available.  
> There's a council meeting Thursday.

And thinks:

> **Hang on. I've got an idea.**

If the system can reward that sort of reasoning, then we're getting somewhere unusual.

---

# The player should get better at the *game*, not memorise the game

This is the solution to your second-playthrough problem.

We want:

> “I understand how this society works much better now.”

We don't want:

> “Go to Greg at 9:00, select option B, sell item X, receive £2 million.”

The distinction is **stable rules, variable situations**.

On a second run, the player should know useful general things. Reliability tends to build trust. Networking matters. Certain institutions behave in predictable ways. Preparation improves professional opportunities. Pushing somebody when they're angry is dangerous. Money creates leverage. Reputation travels.

But the exact **position** should be different.

Maybe the person who could introduce you to an investor this time has fallen out with them. Perhaps the job opportunity appears in another field. A character gets into a relationship earlier. Someone moves away. A business survives instead of failing. Your friendship network develops differently. The economic environment changes.

It's closer to learning chess than learning a puzzle game's solution.

You get stronger as a player because you understand the rules.

But you still have to play the position in front of you.

---

# This radically changes how I think wealth should work

I don't think excellent play should directly cause:

> **Skill → money → more skill → enormous money.**

That inevitably creates an optimal economic strategy.

Instead, player skill should produce **readiness for opportunities**.

There are four ingredients:

**Preparation + execution + circumstances + luck.**

The player can strongly control the first two.

They can partially influence the third.

They cannot completely control the fourth.

So someone who plays extremely well might consistently create good opportunities, recover intelligently from setbacks and capitalise brilliantly when luck appears.

But they cannot press the **Become Millionaire** sequence.

That feels right.

---

# There should be a huge difference between getting comfortable and becoming seriously rich

I would make **financial security an achievable reward for good play**.

If the player manages their career, spending, relationships and opportunities intelligently, they should reasonably be able to stop living on the edge.

That is satisfying progression.

Becoming affluent can require considerably more.

Becoming genuinely wealthy should require some combination of exceptional success, positioning and favourable circumstances.

Becoming **filthy rich** should probably be an unusual life trajectory rather than something a competent player expects every run.

So skill could move the probability distribution.

A poor player repeatedly squanders opportunities.

A decent player builds a stable life.

A strong player creates lots of good opportunities.

An excellent player becomes extraordinarily good at exploiting whatever world state they receive.

But even the excellent player doesn't get to demand that the universe produce an inheritance, successful startup or property windfall.

---

# Luck should change the story, not tell you whether you played well

This is crucial to solving save-scumming.

If:

> 25% chance = £1,000,000  
> 75% chance = nothing

then people will reload.

Of course they will.

We've made one result objectively superior.

Instead, uncertainty should usually cause **branching consequences**.

You apply for some prestigious opportunity.

Success gives you access to one social world.

Failure might cause another person to notice you, free up time for something else, damage a relationship, reveal something, or send you down a completely different trajectory.

Success can still be desirable. Failure doesn't have to secretly be a reward.

But it shouldn't mean:

> “You rolled badly. Your playthrough is now the inferior version.”

The player should often think:

> “Oh shit. Okay. What do I do now?”

rather than immediately reach for Load Game.

---

# We can also technically eliminate most save-scumming

For important uncertainty, **reloading shouldn't produce a new roll**.

The campaign can generate hidden contingencies from its world seed and history.

If an opportunity exists, its uncertain components are effectively already determined before the player presses the button.

Reloading five seconds beforehand gives the same result.

To alter the outcome, the player must alter something meaningful:

prepare better, involve another character, delay the attempt, acquire information, change strategy, improve the relationship, or abandon it.

That means Save/Load remains available for convenience and experimentation without turning into:

> F5 → click → fail → F9 → click → fail → F9 → click → jackpot.

And I wouldn't show giant percentage values everywhere either. Something like:

**Very promising / uncertain / risky / ridiculous**

is probably more appropriate to the game's tone than “73% success probability.”

>   
> the moment the solicitor opens the envelope.

The underlying family circumstances could have been generated when the campaign began.

The player is merely **discovering something that was already true**.

Likewise, whether some business owner is considering retirement, whether land is about to be redeveloped, whether an NPC's family has connections somewhere, or whether a particular company is struggling can exist as hidden world state.

This makes the world feel causal rather than slot-machine-like.

I don't think repetitive grinding fits this game at all.

If the optimal strategy becomes:

> Work café shift 73 times → Social Skill +12 → Money +£4,200

we've undermined what is interesting about it.

The first few times you work somewhere may produce meaningful scenes because you're meeting colleagues and learning the environment.

> **You are now competent at this job.**

Routine work can happen largely in the background unless something interesting occurs.

Likewise exercising, studying, practising an instrument, cooking, etc. shouldn't reward infinite repetition.

You can't do everything.

Someone who's socially isolated but suddenly inherits money gets a completely different story.

Someone already ambitious can invest it.

Someone community-minded can use it locally.

Someone status-conscious can aggressively enter new social circles.

Someone cautious might barely alter their lifestyle.

Someone reckless can absolutely make a catastrophic mess of it.

Therefore the wealth event itself doesn't determine the second half.

**Who the player has become before receiving it determines what wealth means.**

That is considerably more interesting.

## I think we can establish some provisional design rules

1. **No universal victory currency.** Wealth, status and relationships remain separate resources.
2. **Progress primarily expands agency and option space.**
3. **Routine repetition gives rapidly diminishing returns.**
4. **Large outcomes require accumulated world state, not memorised dialogue sequences.**
5. **Player skill improves preparation, decision quality and resilience—not control over fate.**
6. **Important randomness is persistent across reloads.**
7. **Luck creates trajectories rather than simply awarding prizes.**
8. **Extreme wealth is possible, but never the dependable reward for competent play.**
9. **Major commitments consume time and therefore exclude other possibilities.**
10. **A great playthrough means creating an interesting and effective life, not maxing every system.**

The part I particularly like is that this gives *Managed Decline* something much stronger than a traditional progression curve.

At the beginning:

> **The player is mostly navigating circumstances created by other people.**

Much later:

> **The player has accumulated enough knowledge, relationships, resources and credibility that they can start creating circumstances for other people.**

That is a very satisfying progression arc, and it works whether the protagonist ends up with £8,000, £800,000 or £8 million.

I think **that** is what we should prototype before Rich Britain. We should build one small scenario that can be solved very differently by an inexperienced protagonist versus the same protagonist after accumulating relationships, knowledge, money and reputation. If that transformation feels satisfying without XP grinding, we have the core progression model the rest of the game can sit on.

## On the legal concern

There is a useful distinction between **depicting controversial political activity** and actually producing material intended to encourage criminal conduct.

Under the Serious Crime Act 2007, England and Wales have offences relating to intentionally encouraging or assisting offences, while public-order law separately addresses violence, intimidation and certain forms of stirring up hatred. CPS guidance also explicitly distinguishes peaceful protest from conduct involving public-order offending, although protest law itself contains substantial restrictions and has continued changing. ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/27/pdfs/ukpga_20070027_en.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So I would adopt a conservative design boundary:

> **The game's constructive agency systems are built around lawful civic, social, commercial and personal action.**

Petitions, attending meetings, volunteering, journalism, helping businesses, community organisation, lawful campaigning, negotiation, philanthropy, starting organisations, contacting representatives, helping individuals, using professional skills—plenty of comedy and conflict exists there.

Characters can still *discuss*, parody or even engage in bad behaviour where narratively appropriate. But I would be much more cautious about making realistic unlawful disruption, violence, intimidation, hatred or similar conduct into **instructional player mechanics presented as the effective solution**. UK public-order law expressly addresses violence/intimidation, and there are specific offences concerning stirring up hatred. ([cps.gov.uk](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/public-order-offences-incorporating-charging-standard))

For a commercial release, we'd still want a UK media/games solicitor to review particular high-risk storylines rather than assume a general design rule guarantees compliance.

# 6. We need a very small layer of “common sense”

This is probably one of the most important technical design concepts we've reached.

We don't need an LLM reasoning freely about everything humans could possibly do.

We need a **bounded common-sense ontology**.

The game understands basic concepts such as:

### Physical

- people and objects have locations;
- some objects are portable;
- heavy things require transport/help;
- buildings contain spaces;
- damaged things can sometimes be repaired;
- people require time to travel.

### Ownership and access

- things belong to somebody;
- owning something generally permits its use;
- borrowing requires permission;
- venues can be open/closed/restricted;
- organisations control certain resources.

### Human capability

- people can only competently perform things they know how to do;
- multiple people can cooperate;
- tools can enable actions;
- expertise can be hired or requested.

### Social

- people can be asked;
- willingness depends on relationship, personality, interests, commitments and circumstances;
- introductions connect people;
- promises create expectations.

### Economic

- goods/services can cost money;
- resources can be bought, hired, borrowed or provided by someone else;
- money spent is no longer available.

### Temporal

- things take time;
- people can be busy;
- some opportunities expire;
- learning usually takes much longer than executing a familiar action.

That's enough “common sense” to produce an enormous amount of gameplay.

A character has:

**Desires** — what they care about.

**Commitments** — what they're spending their life on.

**Capabilities** — what they can draw upon.

**Knowledge** — what possibilities they can currently recognise.

**Intentions** — what they're trying to make happen now.

# Some things I would actively avoid

Activities become dangerous when they demand an entire bespoke game system.

I would be very wary of:

### Full sports simulations

The protagonist can play football, tennis, golf, etc.

But we shouldn't build FIFA, TopSpin or PGA Tour inside *Managed Decline*.

A match can be represented through choices, scenes and outcomes.

### Detailed crafting

No:

> Iron Ore × 4  
> Wood × 2  
> Craft Superior Bookshelf

DIY can exist without becoming *Minecraft*.

### Deep cooking mechanics

Cooking is excellent socially.

A twenty-ingredient cooking minigame probably isn't.

### Detailed exercise optimisation

No protein/macronutrient spreadsheet.

The player chooses to train regularly and occasionally experiences relevant scenes.

### Hundreds of collectible objects

Objects should matter because they're useful, expressive or socially meaningful—not because every mug needs an inventory slot.

### Simulation of every profession

Jobs should share common mechanics.

We can write distinctive workplaces without implementing a complete accounting simulator, medical simulator, legal simulator and retail simulator.

---

# A useful inclusion test

For any proposed hobby, ask four questions.

**Does it introduce interesting people?**

**Does it create interesting places or scenes?**

**Can it later become useful in unexpected situations?**

**Does it reveal something interesting about Britain or the protagonist?**

If it satisfies **two or more**, it probably deserves inclusion.

If it satisfies all four, it may deserve significant support.

If the only argument is:

> “People do this in real life.”

that's not enough.

---

# I'd deliberately favour socially connective hobbies

The strongest activities for this game are those that naturally create relationships.

For example:

**Excellent**
- band/music
- gym
- football
- amateur theatre
- volunteering
- pub quiz
- community gardening
- cooking/hosting
- hiking group

**Harder to justify deeply**
- stamp collecting
- solo model building
- solitary chess puzzles
- home aquariums

The second group can still exist as character flavour. They just don't justify lots of gameplay unless a particular character/story makes them relevant.

---

# Some apparently mundane hobbies could be disproportionately useful

Because this is *Managed Decline*, I wouldn't only select fashionable activities.

Things like:

- allotments
- amateur dramatics
- pub quizzes
- rambling
- local history
- Sunday league football
- gardening
- volunteering at some bizarre local institution
- choir
- community radio

could give the game considerably more British identity than generic RPG activities.

And they naturally create eccentric ensembles of NPCs.

---

# Don't make every protagonist capable of everything

This is another major scope control.

A player might become heavily involved in:

> music + fitness + career

while another becomes:

> cooking + community activity + photography.

We don't need equivalent depth everywhere in a single run.

The game can contain many potential activities because **the player only deeply engages with a few**.

That is much cheaper than making every hobby something the player is expected to complete.

---

## A reasonable first full-game target

I'd currently aim for roughly:

**5–7 deep activity families**, perhaps another **10–15 lightweight ones**, with everything else implied or used as character flavour.

That's already enough for players to create dramatically different lives.

And I'd avoid publishing a giant hobby list during development. Every activity should have to **earn its way into the game by producing good scenes or useful interactions**.

The test isn't “can we simulate this?”

It's:

> **Will choosing this over spending Saturday somewhere else produce a story the player is glad they experienced?**

That's probably the right scope boundary for *Managed Decline*.

## Assistant

Yes. I think **world-changing action should be one of the major late-game rewards for accumulating capability**. Otherwise all that agency eventually bottoms out at “I can play piano and help Darren move house.”

But “world changing” should usually mean **changing the persistent social world the player actually inhabits**, not controlling Britain from a strategy-game interface.

## A simple scale

### 1. Change a person's life

Available relatively early.

- help someone get a job
- introduce two people who later become important to each other
- help someone leave a bad situation
- teach or mentor somebody
- finance someone's opportunity
- persuade somebody to try something

These can have surprisingly large downstream consequences.

### 2. Change a place or group

This should become increasingly possible once the player is established.

- save or transform a pub/community venue
- start a business
- improve a workplace
- organise a recurring event
- start a club, band, charity or community organisation
- renovate a building
- create something that becomes locally important
- organise people around a practical problem

Now the **map and NPC routines actually change**.

A boarded-up shop becoming a thriving café is much more satisfying than `Community Influence +10`.

### 3. Change an institution or local system

Harder, longer projects.

- become influential inside a company
- take over or reform an organisation
- establish a successful local enterprise
- run a serious campaign around a local issue
- create a publication/media project
- influence a council decision through lawful civic channels
- fund or establish something substantial
- expose incompetence or wrongdoing and cause consequences

These should require combinations of relationships, reputation, money, competence and persistence.

### 4. Occasionally have wider influence

This should be possible but exceptional.

A player's:

- company becomes nationally successful;
- music/art becomes culturally prominent;
- journalism breaks a major story;
- organisation expands;
- invention/project becomes successful;
- political or civic activity becomes influential;
- wealth gives them serious philanthropic/business power.

At that point the player hasn't unlocked **Britain Management Mode**.

Instead, the existing world starts reacting to something they created.

That's much more interesting.

---

# Activities themselves can become influential

This is another reason hobbies shouldn't be treated as dead-end side content.

Someone learns music because they enjoy it.

Later:

> band → local gigs → following → significant career → cultural influence.

Someone enjoys cooking:

> dinners → pop-up → restaurant → local institution → business.

Someone gets interested in journalism:

> small writing → local reporting → investigation → major consequences.

Someone starts volunteering:

> becomes trusted → takes responsibility → helps build an organisation.

So we don't necessarily need a separate **Influence activity**.

Ordinary pursuits can **scale** if the player commits enough life to them and circumstances cooperate.

---

# But influence needs strong costs

Otherwise the optimal strategy becomes:

> Become important as quickly as possible.

Large interventions should consume things players genuinely value:

- **time**
- **money**
- **attention**
- **relationships**
- **reputation**
- **opportunity to pursue other lives**

Running a successful business might mean missing huge parts of your friends' lives.

Leading a community project may consume months.

Trying to influence an institution may create enemies.

Becoming famous can change existing relationships.

That makes influence a **choice of life**, not an upgrade.

---

# And failure should be interesting

A player spends a year trying to save something.

Maybe they fail.

But perhaps:

- they form several important relationships;
- somebody discovers a new career;
- a different organisation emerges;
- the building closes but the community relocates;
- the player learns something that leads elsewhere.

This is important because otherwise major projects become puzzles players reload until they “win.”

---

## I would make one activity family explicitly about this

Alongside work, hobbies, relationships, etc., have something like:

> **Projects**

A Project is simply **something the player chooses to make happen in the world**.

Tiny:

> organise Chloe's birthday properly.

Medium:

> put on a local music night.

Large:

> keep the venue alive.

Very large:

> turn it into a successful cultural institution.

The mechanics underneath remain the same:

**people + competence + money/resources + time + decisions.**

That gives *Managed Decline* a natural escalation:

> **First you learn to run your own life.  
> Then you discover you can sometimes change somebody else's.  
> Eventually you realise you might actually be able to change part of the world around you.**

I think that's probably the strongest form of progression we've identified so far. It gives the player something substantial to strive toward without making wealth, political power, fame, or any other single outcome the game's universal end goal.
