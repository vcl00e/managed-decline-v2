# Character Personalisation, Routines, Young Adult Social Life and Creative Crafts

## User

What are good ways to allow players to personalise their characters in Managed Decline? We've discussed pets, home, wardrobe. what else are a good synergy and worthwhile to implement?

## Assistant

The best additions are the ones that **personalise the player while simultaneously feeding the emergent narrative**. I would avoid customisation that exists only as a menu full of cosmetics.

For *Managed Decline*, I would prioritise these:

| Personalisation area | Why it fits | Systemic payoff | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal tastes** | Music, food, pubs/cafés, books, films, football, hobbies, aesthetics | NPC affinity, invitations, dialogue, gifts, places you naturally gravitate toward | **Very high** |
| **Habits & routines** | Morning coffee, takeaway night, gym, pub after work, Sunday roast, doomscrolling, gardening | Creates recurring situations and lets characters learn who you are | **Very high** |
| **Phone / digital life** | Wallpaper, group chats, contacts, apps, social profile, muted people | Excellent interface for relationships, gossip, invitations and satire | **Very high** |
| **Transport** | Walk, bike, bus, train, cheap car, nice car | Changes where you go, encounters, inconvenience and social perception | **High** |
| **Food & cooking** | Favourite meals, cooking ability, takeaway habits, hosting | Social activity + domestic storytelling + gifts + dates | **High** |
| **Personal possessions** | A few meaningful objects rather than inventory junk | Objects acquire history and can become story props | **High** |
| **Preferred places** | Your café, pub, park bench, corner shop, library, takeaway | Makes the town feel personally yours | **High** |
| **Appearance / grooming** | Hair, makeup, facial hair, tattoos, accessories | Visible identity without needing hundreds of clothing systems | **High** |
| **Background / upbringing** | Where you grew up, education, previous jobs, family situation | Gives dialogue and social situations context | **High, but mostly narrative** |
| **Creative expression** | Photography, music, writing, drawing, DIY etc. | Gives the player ways to leave traces in the world | **Medium–high** |
| **Collections** | Records, books, mugs, football memorabilia, plants | Strong flavour but weak gameplay unless tied into characters | **Medium** |

A particularly strong direction is to think of the player as gradually building a **personal ecosystem** rather than filling out a character sheet.

### Personal tastes

Don't make the player answer a personality questionnaire at character creation. Let the game infer tastes from behaviour.

If they repeatedly:
- order the same thing,
- listen to a certain kind of music,
- visit the same pub,
- watch terrible reality television,
- go birdwatching,
- refuse every invitation involving sport,

the game gradually treats those things as part of their identity.

Then NPCs can say things like:

> “I saw that absolutely dreadful little café you like has reopened.”

That is much more satisfying than `Favourite Food: Curry +2`.

It also gives the simulation useful information for **generating relevant opportunities**. Someone who has demonstrated an interest in photography might hear about a local exhibition; someone who likes football might be invited to watch a match.

---

## Routines could be one of the strongest forms of personalisation

This fits very closely with the design we've already discussed around avoiding repetitive work simulation.

The player might organically establish things such as:

- coffee before work
- Friday drinks
- taking the dog through a certain park
- calling their mum on Sundays
- getting the same takeaway
- going running after stressful days
- reading in the library
- sitting in the same café when avoiding everyone

The simulation can recognise these as **soft routines**, not mandatory schedules.

Once established, routines become narrative infrastructure.

If the player always goes to the same café on Saturday morning, the game has a believable place to:
- introduce someone,
- interrupt the routine,
- show social change,
- let an NPC deliberately seek them out,
- make the café closing actually matter.

That is unusually good synergy.

---

## The player's phone should probably be highly personal

Not in the sense of building an elaborate fake smartphone operating system.

Just enough that it gradually becomes **their phone**.

For example:

- wallpaper
- pinned conversations
- group chats
- contacts/nicknames
- muted people
- favourite photos
- social-media profile
- playlists
- calendar fragments
- saved places

This is useful because the phone is already an excellent delivery mechanism for the emergent game:

**relationships → messages → invitations → gossip → news → opportunities → consequences**

Personalisation therefore costs relatively little while touching a huge amount of the game.

You could even have the player's photo gallery gradually become a lightweight **record of their playthrough**.

---

## Give players "their places"

This may be more important than giving them dozens of possessions.

Over time the player should acquire places they think of as:

**my pub  
my café  
my corner shop  
my park  
my takeaway  
my barber  
my quiet place**

Some might even become unofficial social territory.

Different characters begin showing up there because they know the player does.

A player who spends every evening at a slightly grim working men's club should end up with a very different social world from one who keeps appearing at the arts centre café.

And crucially, none of this requires explicit character stats.

---

## Personal possessions should be scarce but meaningful

I would **not** implement a Bethesda-style junk inventory.

Instead, allow a relatively small number of objects to become significant:

- grandfather's watch
- terrible mug someone gave you
- expensive camera
- battered guitar
- football scarf
- ridiculous souvenir
- framed photograph
- old laptop
- bicycle
- plant someone asked you to look after

Objects can accumulate associations.

The guitar isn't interesting because it has `Quality 63`.

It's interesting because:

> Alice gave it to you after that disastrous night in Brighton.

That gives the generative narrative system **persistent physical anchors** it can reuse later.

This idea also meshes naturally with the home system.

---

# One especially useful concept: **identity through use**

I think this should be the governing design principle.

Don't ask:

> What can the player customise?

Ask:

> What can the game notice about how this particular player lives?

Wardrobe works because the player **wears it**.

A home works because the player **lives in it**.

A pet works because the player **cares for it and other people react to it**.

The same test eliminates a lot of feature creep.

For instance:

**Custom phone wallpaper:** cheap and repeatedly visible → worthwhile.

**Detailed furniture rotation and placement:** expensive, little narrative consequence → probably not.

**Favourite pub:** extremely cheap conceptually and creates dozens of narrative possibilities → excellent.

**Character nose-width slider:** technically possible but contributes almost nothing to the core experience → basic presets may be enough.

---

# I would build the player's personal identity around about seven surfaces

Not seven menu categories—seven things the simulation understands:

1. **How I look** — body/face basics, hair, grooming, wardrobe.
2. **Where I live** — home, possessions, pet.
3. **Where I go** — favourite places and habitual routes.
4. **What I like** — food, music, culture, hobbies, entertainment.
5. **What I do** — activities, skills and routines.
6. **Who matters to me** — friends, family, romantic relationships, rivals, communities.
7. **How I present myself** — phone, social persona, manners, reputation and behaviour.

That is already enough combinatorial variation for two players to develop characters that feel **radically different**, even if they began with nearly identical avatars.

And importantly, almost every one of these feeds directly into *Managed Decline's* real game: **relationships, social situations, opportunities, comedy, belonging, conflict and discovering what you care about**.

I would therefore treat **routines, tastes, personal places and the phone** as the best additions beyond pets/home/wardrobe. They give substantially more narrative leverage per unit of implementation work than deeper cosmetic customisation.

## Assistant

Yes. I think there is a coherent design here, but it requires separating **character identity**, **player behaviour**, and **repetitive life simulation**. If those blur together, the game starts trying to simulate a person rather than creating an entertaining fictional life.

# 1. Background/upbringing: don't choose between "menu" and "random"

Use a **small authored background builder with a Surprise Me button**.

I would not ask the player to fill in a biography. Give them perhaps 3–5 consequential anchors:

- Where/how they grew up.
- What they were doing immediately before the game.
- Broad family situation.
- Broad economic/living situation.
- One socially relevant past connection or formative fact.

These should be things characters can actually talk about.

For example:

> **Before moving here, you were…**  
> Working an unremarkable office job  
> Just out of university  
> Working hospitality/retail  
> Between things  
> **Surprise me**

Then the game assembles a short natural-language history.

Crucially, **leave lots of the past undefined**.

If nobody has ever established whether you played football at school, the game doesn't need to know. Later, a conversation could let the player establish:

> “Actually, I used to play.”  
> “Absolutely not.”  
> “I was the child mysteriously excused from PE every week.”

That fact then becomes canon.

This gives you an expandable **retroactive biography** without a fifty-question character creator.

`Surprise me` should generate from the same authored components rather than inventing arbitrary LLM biography. The player can reroll individual elements.

---

# 2. Physical appearance: yes, but keep it qualitative

I think the useful parameters are:

- short / average / tall
- several broad body builds
- skin tone
- hair colour
- hairstyle
- perhaps facial hair
- later: clothes, glasses, jewellery, hats, tattoos etc.

I'd use **build**, rather than asking players for "weight". You don't need kilograms or precise body sliders.

The goal isn't anatomical simulation. It's:

> **Can I immediately recognise my character when they are standing among six other little people?**

That's the important test.

## Can height/body build work with 2.5D flat characters?

Yes, but I wouldn't simply stretch the finished image.

Vertical scaling like this:

`[whole character image] → 0.90× / 1.00× / 1.10×`

is extremely cheap, but eventually starts looking like someone resized a PNG.

And horizontal deformation for body weight is worse. Clothing, limbs, heads and accessories all start distorting.

A better implementation is a **rigged paper-doll character**.

Conceptually:

```text
hair
head
neck
torso + clothing
arms + sleeves
hands
legs + trousers/skirt
shoes
accessories
```

These layers share a small skeletal rig.

Height can then come mainly from:

- leg length
- torso length
- overall scale

Build can come from:

- torso width
- hip/shoulder width
- limb thickness

You only need a limited safe deformation range.

An even simpler solution is probably appropriate for MD:

**3 height bands × perhaps 4 body-base archetypes.**

Clothing is then rigged/deformed within those bases.

That gives meaningful variation without turning avatar technology into a project of its own.

# 3. A faceless protagonist could actually be a very good fit

With an important condition:

**don't use the player's face for emotional acting.**

If the player has a blank face while everyone else gets elaborate VN close-ups, emotional scenes could become bizarre.

Instead I would separate presentation.

### World scenes

The player is the little customisable faceless character:

- hair
- silhouette
- clothing
- posture
- animations
- accessories
- ridiculous outfits

They can be extremely recognisable without facial features.

### VN/dialogue scenes

Don't put a big blank portrait of the protagonist on screen.

The NPC provides the facial acting. The player's presence can instead be communicated through:

- dialogue choices
- camera position
- occasional hands/body shots
- world-avatar reaction animations
- framing and sound

That could give MD quite a distinctive visual language.

A faceless player character also has one major practical advantage:

**hairstyles and clothes become much more visually important.**

Which is precisely where you want customisation effort going.

# 4. What should the avatar actually accomplish?

This matters more than feature count.

I would give the avatar six jobs.

### Recognition

> That's me.

The player should instantly locate themselves in the diorama.

### Self-expression

> That's how I've decided my character looks today.

Wardrobe, hairstyle and accessories handle most of this.

### Social signalling

Characters can notice things:

> “Are you seriously wearing that to the council meeting?”

This gives cosmetics gameplay value without making them RPG equipment.

### Physical comedy

The avatar needs enough animation for:

- collapsing into a chair
- awkwardly standing around
- drunken dancing
- running for the bus
- holding enormous objects
- wearing inappropriate clothing
- getting soaked
- being dragged somewhere by a dog

This probably matters **more than facial customisation**.

### Continuity

The player appears consistently:

- walking around
- in photographs
- at events
- in group scenes
- at home
- perhaps in phone photos/social media

### Low production multiplication

Adding a coat should not require drawing every scene again.

That should be a hard architectural requirement.

So yes: **I think MD should have an avatar**, but it should be a highly modular world avatar, not a heroic RPG character-creation system.

# 5. Player tastes: don't try to identify the human player's tastes

Your piano example exposes the problem perfectly.

Suppose I love piano in real life.

Tonight I've opened *Managed Decline* because I want to:

> ruin a planning committee meeting and seduce the deputy manager of a garden centre.

My refusal to practise piano tells the game absolutely nothing about whether I like piano.

So don't model:

> **Player likes piano.**

Model:

> **Player currently chooses piano-related content when it competes with other MD content.**

That's an **in-game revealed preference**, not a personality profile.

There's another useful distinction.

## The character can have tastes independent of player behaviour

Imagine that I establish:

> Charlotte loves classical music.

But then I spend six in-game months never going near a piano.

That's not a contradiction.

It's actually very human.

Maybe Charlotte:

- loves listening but hates practising;
- thinks she ought to learn;
- keeps meaning to;
- likes the identity of being musical;
- simply has more important things happening.

An NPC could eventually say:

> “Didn't you buy that keyboard three months ago?”

That is **better characterisation** than having the game secretly conclude that I dislike music.

So I'd maintain two concepts:

**Established character preferences**  
Facts about the fictional character.

**Observed play interests**  
What kinds of game opportunities the human player keeps engaging with.

The first creates fiction.

The second helps the director decide what content to surface.

Don't pretend they're the same thing.

---

# 6. This solves routines too

You're right: virtually nobody will think:

> It's 08:13. I ought to click **Drink Coffee** for immersion.

And they shouldn't have to.

I think MD should use an important rule:

> **Routine is established interactively, then simulated automatically until something interesting happens.**

For example, perhaps you've visited the café several mornings.

Eventually:

> **This is becoming your usual morning stop.**  
> Make it a regular thing / Not really

Choose yes.

You do **not** subsequently walk there and order a flat white every morning.

The game now understands:

> On ordinary mornings, Alex often gets coffee here.

That becomes narrative infrastructure.

One morning:

> The shutters are down.

Now you play it.

Another morning:

> Tabitha is sitting in your usual seat.

Now you play it.

Another:

> The barista tells you someone was asking about you.

Play it.

The thirty mundane coffees between these scenes don't need to exist as gameplay.

This makes routines useful without turning MD into *The Sims*.

---

# 7. Diegetic character preferences are useful for exactly this reason

Give the character **soft preferences**, never needs meters.

For instance:

> prefers tea  
> loves greasy takeaways  
> hates driving  
> enjoys cooking for people  
> feels uncomfortable in expensive restaurants

These don't make decisions for the player.

They change:

- narration;
- dialogue;
- available jokes;
- NPC expectations;
- spontaneous opportunities;
- how mundane situations are framed.

The player can always contradict them.

And contradiction itself can become character development.

> You always claimed you hated fancy restaurants. You've been here three times this month.

Excellent.

The game isn't enforcing a personality.

It's **remembering one**.

---

# 8. Apply the same principle to places, transport, food and grooming

Don't make these chores.

### Transport

The player might establish:

> usually takes the bus

Then ordinary transport disappears into scene transitions.

But the game can use it to produce:

- bus encounters;
- delays;
- overheard conversations;
- missing the last bus;
- someone offering a lift;
- eventually buying a car and changing their social geography.

### Food

Don't simulate breakfast.

Remember:

> favourite takeaway  
> terrible cook  
> always orders the same curry  
> weirdly serious about coffee

Then use those facts when food becomes narratively relevant.

### Grooming

Assume ordinary maintenance happens invisibly.

Make it interactive when the player actually wants to **change their look**.

And yes, ridiculous appearances are worth supporting because they're one of the rare forms of personalisation where the player can deliberately create comedy.

NPC reaction should be contextual rather than:

`Ridiculous Hat → -3 Respect`

Someone may love it.

Someone may be appalled.

Someone may pretend not to notice.

Someone may desperately ask to borrow it.

That's much more MD.

# 9. Creative crafts: I think you've found something genuinely valuable

But I would avoid building:

> Photography minigame  
> Drawing minigame  
> Poetry minigame  
> Music minigame  
> Writing minigame

as five independent systems.

Instead build one underlying concept:

# **Creative artefacts**

The player creates a small thing.

It becomes an actual object in the fiction.

It can then be:

- shown to someone;
- performed;
- sent through the phone;
- posted;
- displayed at home;
- gifted;
- mocked;
- treasured;
- remembered;
- brought up months later.

That persistence is what justifies the mechanic.

---

# Photography is probably the strongest first implementation

The diorama environment does most of the work for you.

Player controls:

- position
- angle
- zoom
- subject
- perhaps focus/exposure at most

Take picture.

Done.

The game can understand contextual metadata without computer vision because it already knows:

> Characters in frame: Tabitha, Dan  
> Location: bus shelter  
> Event: protest aftermath  
> Time: night  
> Weather: rain

So NPCs can react meaningfully to the photograph.

And the image itself is genuinely **the player's creation**.

Store it in the phone.

Send it.

Frame it at home.

Put it in a local exhibition.

Use it as phone wallpaper.

Now several customisation systems suddenly connect.

---

# Drawing could intentionally be a stupid little creative toy

Your instinct here is good.

Rather than implementing sophisticated art software, give the player deliberately constrained tools.

For instance:

- six pen strokes;
- a few shapes;
- stamps;
- colours;
- arrows;
- text labels;
- stickers.

Now somebody says:

> “Draw what you think the new leisure centre should look like.”

The player's limited tools almost guarantee some degree of incompetence.

But they still have genuine authorship.

And because the game knows the prompt, it doesn't need to understand the drawing perfectly.

Party drawing games become obvious:

> Draw Gary from memory.

Everyone's results can be awful.

That is the point.

---

# Writing and poetry should probably work through constrained composition

Free-text writing creates a massive interpretation problem.

Instead, give players interesting components.

Imagine composing a terrible local-election poem by choosing:

**Subject**

> Britain  
> The high street  
> Sandra  
> Potholes

**Opening image**

> rain on a Tesco trolley  
> a dying shopping centre  
> two foxes fighting  
> the number 47 bus

**Emotional direction**

> sincere  
> furious  
> romantic  
> embarrassingly grandiose

Then perhaps let the player choose/rearrange lines generated from an authored vocabulary.

The result can be funny **and meaningfully theirs**.

The same system could make:

- birthday cards;
- protest signs;
- terrible speeches;
- dating messages;
- local newspaper letters;
- song lyrics.

That's much more valuable than building a standalone poetry simulator.

---

# Music could use the same philosophy

I would specifically **not** make the main creative system a rhythm game.

Rhythm games test execution.

You want **composition/expression**.

A very promising mechanic would be a tiny musical sequencer where it's difficult to make something completely broken.

For example, the player gets a short repeating phrase:

```text
● — ● — | — ● ● —
```

They can manipulate things like:

- melodic contour ↑ ↓ →
- rhythm
- repetition
- rests
- instrument
- tempo
- perhaps a few chord/mood choices

Keep everything quantised to a compatible musical framework.

Then even musically inexperienced players can produce something recognisable.

Some results will be stupid.

Good.

Imagine playing your dreadful eight-second keyboard riff at:

- an open-mic night;
- someone's birthday;
- a demonstration;
- a funeral where you have spectacularly misjudged the situation.

The important thing isn't:

> MUSIC SKILL: 74

It's what you played, **where**, and **to whom**.

---

# Collaborative music could be even better

This might fit MD particularly well.

An NPC drummer establishes a groove.

Another character adds bass.

The player adds some tiny melodic/rhythmic contribution.

Now you're not asking the player to compose an entire song.

You're asking them:

> **What do you contribute to this moment?**

Their little motif might subsequently recur when the band plays.

Potentially it could even become:

- their ringtone;
- music in someone's amateur video;
- a running joke;
- a recurring musical motif associated with their character.

That creates astonishingly good payoff from a very small input system.

---

# The design test I'd use for every creative activity

A craft is worth implementing when it produces this loop:

**Context → tiny creative act → persistent artefact → other people react → artefact gains history**

Not:

**Activity → skill XP → repeat activity → unlock better activity**

That distinction is important.

Photography passes immediately.

Simple drawing probably passes.

Tiny musical composition probably passes.

Constrained writing/poetry probably passes.

Full painting simulation, instrument-learning simulation, elaborate crafting trees etc. probably don't.

## So the larger personalisation model becomes surprisingly coherent

The game gradually knows:

**What you look like.**  
Avatar/wardrobe.

**Where you came from.**  
Lightweight evolving biography.

**What sort of person your character claims to be.**  
Diegetic tastes/preferences.

**What the human player currently enjoys doing in MD.**  
Observed play interests.

**What your ordinary life looks like.**  
Compressed routines.

**Where you belong.**  
Regular places and people.

**What you've made.**  
Photos, drawings, writing, music.

**What has happened to those things.**  
Memory/history.

That last category is especially interesting because it changes customisation from **selecting content the developers made** into **accumulating evidence of your particular playthrough**.

And I think the **phone becomes the natural hub for much of it**: photographs, messages, favourite places, creative artefacts, contacts, social traces and little fragments of personal history all collecting there.

## Assistant

I would revise the model in two directions:

1. **Routine should be something the game notices, compresses, and later exploits—not something the player schedules.**
2. **Creative crafts do not need to become narratively important artefacts.** Their primary jobs can instead be expression, comedy, British cultural texture, social play, and customisation.

# 1. Establishing routines without adding management

A useful rule is:

> **Experience it → repeat it → the world recognises it → the game compresses it.**

The player should never open a "Routines" menu.

## Stage 1 — The first time is playable

Suppose a coworker asks:

> “Pub after work?”

You actually go. There's a scene, conversations, perhaps something happens.

Later that week, another invitation happens. You go again.

The following Friday someone simply says:

> “You coming?”

At that point the game has enough evidence that this might be becoming a thing.

---

## Stage 2 — Establish recurring behaviour diegetically

Instead of:

> `SET ROUTINE: Friday pub`

the game uses naturally recurring language:

> “Same again next Friday?”

**Yeah.**  
**Probably.**  
**No promises.**  
**Absolutely not.**

That answer establishes different strengths of expectation.

Now the simulation knows that Friday drinks are part of the character's ordinary life.

This is particularly good because **NPCs establish routines with you**.

That's how a lot of real-life routines actually happen.

You don't decide:

> I shall create a weekly social activity with Dave.

Dave keeps asking if you're coming to the pub, and eventually you're part of the pub group.

---

# 2. There should be several ways a routine can become established

Not everything needs repetition.

### Explicit recurrence

Someone says:

> “Same time next week?”

Accepting is enough.

Excellent for:

- clubs;
- regular drinks;
- exercise;
- lessons;
- family visits;
- hobby groups;
- dates;
- volunteering.

### Repeated behaviour

Player independently does something several times.

Eventually the environment acknowledges it.

Barista:

> “Usual?”

The player can simply say yes.

Now it is canon.

### Default imposed by circumstances

A job immediately creates:

- workplace;
- colleagues;
- broad working hours;
- commute assumptions.

The player doesn't need to establish "going to work" manually.

### Relationship-driven routine

A person begins naturally appearing in the character's ordinary life.

For example, after repeatedly eating lunch with Aisha:

> “I've saved you a seat.”

At that point she has become one of your regular lunch people without any formal declaration.

This is much better than maintaining a friendship schedule.

---

# 3. Then stop making the player perform it

Once established:

> **boring repetition disappears.**

If the character normally:

- gets the bus;
- buys coffee;
- works;
- eats lunch with two coworkers;
- gets takeaway Thursday;
- goes to the pub Friday;

the player doesn't have to click through any of that.

Those things fill the **implicit time between playable scenes**.

The game only opens them back up when something changes.

For example:

> You normally get the 43 to work.

No gameplay required.

But today:

> **CANCELLED**

Now the commute becomes gameplay.

Or:

> You normally have lunch with Priya.

Normally skipped.

Then one day:

> Priya sits down and doesn't touch her food.

Now it's a scene.

This is where routine becomes enormously useful to narrative: **normality gives disruption meaning.**

# 4. I think MD needs a "life montage" language

This could solve a lot of problems at once.

Instead of simulating every day, occasional short transitions show glimpses of ordinary life:

- bus arriving;
- takeaway bag;
- office desk;
- dog walk;
- pint on a table;
- rain outside the window;
- player collapsed on sofa;
- regular café;
- someone's group-chat notification.

Perhaps three or four rapid little shots while time passes.

The exact imagery is generated from the routines the character has established.

So two players get different transitions.

One person's week might visually become:

**bike → office → gym → cooking → girlfriend's flat**

Another:

**bus → office → corner shop → pub → takeaway → cat**

That does more to make a fictional life feel personal than repeatedly forcing players to operate those activities.

It also suits the illustrated 2.5D aesthetic extremely well.

# 5. Work should use exactly the same system

Work is particularly dangerous because it can turn into *Job Simulator: Administrative Decline Edition*.

A job establishes a baseline automatically:

> You work here.  
> These people are normally around.  
> These are roughly your hours.

Most work happens offscreen or in montage.

The playable parts are things like:

- unusual customers;
- office politics;
- lunch conversations;
- being asked to cover something;
- arguments;
- rumours;
- absurd management initiative;
- colleague having a crisis;
- union issue;
- someone being fired;
- mysterious email;
- unexpected inspection.

There could occasionally be a **tiny work decision** during time compression:

> Another tedious Thursday afternoon.

**Help Jamie finish something**  
**Pretend to be extremely busy**  
**Disappear for tea**  
**Actually do some work**

But only when it produces characterisation or consequences.

Never because the player has to maintain a "job performance meter."

# 6. Regular places should be socially earned

I wouldn't let the player declare:

> ⭐ Favourite Café

Instead, places acquire status through use.

There could be roughly three invisible states:

**Known → Familiar → Regular**

The player never needs to see those labels.

They experience the difference.

### Known

> “What can I get you?”

### Familiar

> “Back again?”

### Regular

> “Usual?”

Eventually:

> “Dan was looking for you yesterday.”

That last line matters enormously.

The place has changed from **location** into **part of the player's social world**.

Regularity can unlock:

- greetings;
- gossip;
- informal favours;
- knowing staff;
- recognising other customers;
- invitations;
- being trusted with minor things;
- characters knowing where to find you.

No XP bar required.

---

# 7. People should enter your "orbit" the same way

I'd avoid explicitly categorising NPCs as:

> Core Friend  
> Regular Acquaintance  
> Coworker Level 4

Internally, absolutely track frequency and context.

But externally, communicate it through behaviour.

Someone becomes a regular because:

- they message more;
- they start assuming you'll come;
- they show up at your normal places;
- they reference previous conversations;
- mutual acquaintances mention them;
- you develop shorthand;
- they stop explaining basic things to you.

One subtle but powerful transition:

Early:

> “Do you want to come to Leah's thing Saturday?”

Later:

> “We're at Leah's Saturday, obviously.”

That's the game telling the player:

**you have become part of this social group.**

No UI required.

# 8. A useful hidden model

Under the hood, routines can actually be very simple.

Each recurring pattern needs something approximately like:

```text
Context:
Friday after work

Usual action:
Pub

Usual place:
The Railway

People:
Jamie, Priya, sometimes Lewis

Strength:
Established

Skip:
Normally

Interrupt when:
relationship event
location event
special encounter
player deliberately chooses something else
```

That's enough.

The system doesn't need to construct a minute-by-minute life.

It needs to know **what normally happens so it can skip it intelligently**.

# 9. Preferences can populate the harmless gaps automatically

This also gives the diegetic preferences we discussed a clear job.

Suppose the character creator establishes:

- drinks coffee;
- likes cooking;
- hates driving;
- prefers quiet places.

Those don't create gameplay obligations.

They help the game fill in background detail.

A montage might therefore show them making coffee at home instead of grabbing an energy drink.

But **socially consequential routines should require actual play evidence**.

So:

> Likes coffee

doesn't automatically mean:

> Regular at Costa.

The latter has to emerge from the character actually going somewhere.

That's a useful distinction.

# 12. One reusable mechanic could support many crafts

This is important for scope.

Don't build twelve minigames.

Build perhaps **two or three expressive toy systems**, then reskin them.

## A. Collage / composition system

Player gets:

- a base;
- a limited set of shapes/images;
- text;
- stamps;
- stickers;
- crude drawing;
- rotation/scaling.

That one system could produce:

- posters;
- birthday cards;
- protest signs;
- flyers;
- zines;
- T-shirts;
- mugs;
- presentation slides;
- album covers;
- awful logos.

The constraints are part of the humour.

## B. Performance/sequencing system

Could cover:

- music;
- karaoke embellishments;
- poetry performance;
- maybe amateur theatre beats.

For music, I still like giving the player **small compositional control**, but I'd make it sillier and more social.

For example:

A keyboard offers five safe musical fragments:

> dramatic  
> melancholy  
> jaunty  
> ominous  
> suspiciously funk

Player chains three or four fragments, changes tempo and instrument, and performs it.

You don't need a full sequencer.

The interesting result is:

> **you chose to play an inexplicably ominous synth solo during Karen's retirement party.**

The music provides the joke.

The game doesn't need to preserve the melody for twelve hours of narrative consequences.

## C. Physical shaping/decorating system

A very small deformation + decoration mechanic could cover:

- pottery;
- cake decorating;
- snowmen;
- flower arranging;
- perhaps model making.

Add decorations.

Produce something potentially grotesque.

NPCs react immediately.

Done.

That could be extremely funny if the rendering is intentionally tactile and slightly handmade.

# 18. Music should primarily be social

I'd position musical creativity around:

- karaoke;
- amateur bands;
- open-mic nights;
- messing around at someone's house;
- community events;
- terrible office talent shows;
- church/village-hall performances;
- busking.

The player doesn't need to "learn piano because piano is their hobby."

Instead, the interesting question is:

> **What do you do when music becomes part of a social situation?**

Perhaps someone hands you a keyboard.

You can:

**Play something safe**  
**Try to improvise**  
**Attempt the song everyone wants**  
**Play something completely inappropriate**

Then the little composition interface appears if you choose to engage creatively.

# 19. This suggests a better philosophy for hobbies generally

I would not design:

> PLAYER CHOOSES HOBBY → PLAYER MUST SERVICE HOBBY

Instead:

> WORLD CONTAINS CULTURAL ACTIVITIES → PLAYER KEEPS ENGAGING WITH SOME → THEY GRADUALLY BECOME PART OF THEIR LIFE

So the game doesn't ask at character creation:

> What's your creative hobby?

The player goes to a pottery class because Emma invited them.

Maybe they hate it.

Maybe they come back.

If they keep returning, *then* pottery becomes one of their things.

Exactly the same compressed-routine system handles it.

That connects the two design problems.

# The unified system

I think we now have a cleaner architecture:

### **Preferences**
Facts established about the character. Mostly flavour and option weighting.

### **Actions**
What the player actually chooses during meaningful scenes.

### **Patterns**
Repeated actions/accepted invitations establish ordinary life.

### **Compression**
Established ordinary life happens automatically.

### **Interruptions**
Something unusual turns an ordinary pattern back into gameplay.

### **Creative activities**
Occasional expressive toys embedded in those scenes—not separate progression systems.

### **Persistence only where it earns its keep**
Keep something when it:

- changes the avatar;
- decorates the home;
- changes the environment;
- works well on the phone/social feed;
- is inherently funny to encounter again.

Otherwise, the creation can simply have existed in that scene.

That last restriction should save you a lot of unnecessary complexity. It also gives *Managed Decline* a much more convincing texture: the player isn't managing a simulation of daily life; **they are accumulating a recognisable ordinary life in the gaps between extraordinary, awkward and ridiculous things happening to them.**

# 1. First, split "routines" into three things

This solves most of the edge cases.

### Commitments
Things the character has actually promised to attend.

- work shift
- doctor's appointment
- date
- booked escape room
- friend's birthday
- train they've booked

These **must be visible** somewhere because missing them matters.

### Plans
Things that are expected but negotiable.

- Friday dinner with friends
- Sunday at parents'
- weekly club
- "we usually get lunch together"

These can be cancelled without the game treating it like breaking causality.

### Habits
Things the simulation has observed.

- normally takes the 43 to work
- gets coffee from that café
- usually walks the dog through the park
- buys takeaway after late shifts

These should require **zero management**.

That means I no longer think the game needs a "routine summary."

The phone calendar only needs to tell you:

> **Today**  
> Work 09:00  
> Bowling with Maya, Josh, Ellie 19:30

It does **not** need to say:

> 08:12 — Bus 43  
> 08:43 — Flat white  
> 12:20 — Lunch with Priya

Those are simulation knowledge, not player obligations.

# 2. The most important rule: the player's current action always wins

A routine should **never forcibly teleport the player**.

Suppose it's 08:20 and the character normally leaves for work.

### During montage/time compression

Easy:

> home → bus → coffee → office

The whole routine compresses.

### During active free play

Nothing automatically happens.

At the point where it becomes relevant, the game gives an unobtrusive contextual signal:

> **8:20 — You normally leave about now if you want to be at work for nine.**

Continue what you're doing.

Or leave.

That's it.

If you ignore it, eventually:

> **8:55 — You're not making nine o'clock from here.**

Now that has become an actual situation.

The game shouldn't protect the player from their own decisions.

---

# 3. Being somewhere unexpected is where the system gets interesting

Suppose you normally commute:

**Home → walk → Bus 43 → office**

But you've slept at your girlfriend's flat across town.

The game should **not execute the memorised routine**.

The routine is actually conditional:

> When going to work **from home**, normally take the 43.

From somewhere else, ordinary navigation takes over.

At 08:00 the game might know:

> Work starts at 09:00.  
> Current travel time: ~48 minutes.

At 08:15:

> You should probably leave.

At 08:40:

> You are going to be late.

Then normal choices emerge:

- head to work;
- message/call work;
- call in sick;
- continue what you're doing;
- attempt some ridiculous alternative transport solution.

This potentially creates stories **because ordinary life wasn't followed**.

---

# 4. Routine chains should collapse into one contextual pattern

Don't independently simulate:

> wake up  
> get dressed  
> walk to bus  
> catch 43  
> coffee  
> office

Treat it internally as something like:

**Workday arrival pattern**

```text
IF:
working today
starting from home
nothing unusual happening

THEN:
home → 43 bus → café → workplace
```

Usually the montage handles the entire thing.

But any node can fail:

> Bus cancelled.

The compression stops **there**.

Now you're standing at the bus stop.

That is a very useful general mechanic:

> **Compress normality until something stops being normal.**

# 5. Changing a routine should mostly happen by simply changing behaviour

No edit screen.

If I normally get coffee and today I don't:

Nothing happens.

If I stop going for a week:

The habit weakens.

If I start going somewhere else:

A new habit gradually replaces it.

Eventually the old barista might say:

> “Haven't seen you in ages.”

That's vastly better than:

> `Coffee Routine removed.`

Likewise, if you start cycling to work repeatedly, cycling simply becomes your new assumed commute.

# 6. Explicit relationships need explicit cancellation

There is one exception.

If another person expects you somewhere, **you have to deal with them**.

That's not routine management. That's social gameplay.

Group chat:

> **Maya:** still on for bowling tonight?

You can say:

> Yep  
> Going to be late  
> Can't make it  
> Ignore message

All four are legitimate actions.

Likewise quitting a club is different from merely not attending once.

After several absences:

> **Lewis:** You actually coming back to climbing or should I stop booking you in?

Now the game naturally asks whether that recurring plan still exists.

# 7. Work gets stricter rules because it's an obligation

This is desirable.

Work shouldn't automatically drag you there.

It should provide enough information that missing it is knowingly your decision.

So:

**08:15:** ordinary reminder if necessary.

**08:40:** lateness warning.

**09:05:** manager messages.

**09:20:** maybe calls.

And then whatever that particular workplace actually does.

This creates opportunities for the player to be:

- conscientious;

- chaotic;

- unlucky;

Much better than a work-attendance stat.

# 16. British life becomes the source of activity variety

I would deliberately span several textures of contemporary life rather than only fashionable metropolitan activities.

**Ordinary:** cinema, shopping, meal out, cooking at someone's flat, gaming night, park, café.

**Active:** bowling, climbing, skating, swimming, five-a-side, badminton, crazy golf.

**Creative:** pottery painting, karaoke, drawing night, craft class, amateur theatre.

**Culture:** gig, comedy, theatre, museum late, gallery, book event, festival.

**Very British:** pub quiz, seaside day trip, car-boot sale, charity-shop crawl, local fête, bonfire night, football/non-league match, panto, garden centre inexplicably becoming a whole afternoon.

**Commercial nonsense:** immersive experience, themed bottomless something, aggressively Instagrammable venue, overpriced competitive socialising.

**Community:** volunteering, fundraiser, community garden, local event.

**At home:** dinner party, takeaway and film, board games, pre-drinks, birthday, helping someone move house.

And occasionally:

> **The plan was perfectly reasonable. Britain intervened.**

Rail replacement bus.

Horizontal rain.

Venue unexpectedly shut.

Everyone accidentally goes to two branches of the same chain.

Booking system insists there is no reservation.

This is where the *Managed Decline* layer sits on top of genuine social warmth.

# 17. The activity should create circumstances, not necessarily jokes

This is important tonally.

A pottery-painting trip with four friends doesn't have to become absurd.

It might simply be pleasant.

Someone makes an awful mug.

Someone reveals something personal.

Two people quietly flirt.

Someone is surprisingly talented.

Then everyone gets chips.

Those genuinely nice sequences give the satire contrast.

Otherwise MD risks implying:

> British life is relentlessly miserable and everyone is ridiculous.

The stronger underlying message is closer to:

> **Life is frequently absurd, institutions are frequently maddening, but people still manage to have a bloody good time together.**

That feels much closer to the game we've been designing.

And it gives the player a genuine reason to protect friendships, neighbourhoods and places rather than merely observing their decline.

## I think the core life loop is becoming clearer

**Make/receive plans → play the interesting social experience → ordinary life settles into patterns → montage compresses it → something changes or disrupts it → player responds → relationships and routines evolve → make more plans.**

The player isn't wandering around looking for stories.

They're **living a busy young-adult life**, and the stories emerge from what they agreed to do, who they did it with, what went wrong, and what became normal afterward.
