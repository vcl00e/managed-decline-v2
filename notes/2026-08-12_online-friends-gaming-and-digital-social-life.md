# Online Friends, Gaming and Digital Social Life

## User

Would is make sense for Managed decline to have a online friends and online gaming activities? think discord communities and discord friends playing games, talking about their lives and watching movies together online. is that a significant part of youth culture in britain? would that add or take things away from the game?

## Assistant

Yes. I think **online friendship and online hanging out belongs in Managed Decline**, and excluding it would actually make its portrayal of contemporary young-adult life feel incomplete.

There is strong evidence that this is mainstream rather than a niche “gamer” subculture. Ofcom’s 2026 research says **89% of UK 16–24-year-olds play games**, while 97% of 16–34-year-old internet users use social media. Ofcom also found in June 2026 that **79% of UK adults have used an online community**, alongside 78% of 13–17-year-olds. Earlier Ofcom work found that online multiplayer participation is especially high among young adults, and its qualitative research describes gaming as both entertainment and a social activity. The Government's 2025 *Youth Matters* work also explicitly describes young people's lives as shaped by an “always-connected digital world”, while identifying community, connection and belonging as important concerns.

But I would **not add “online gaming” as another big gameplay system**.

## Treat the voice call as a place

The useful conceptual shift is:

> A Discord call is effectively another location in the world.

Just as the player can go to the pub, someone's flat, a café or a community centre, they can **join the call**.

The player opens their computer or phone and sees something like:

**The Rat Hole — Voice**  
🟢 Jamie  
🟢 Niamh  
🟢 `xX_ToasterBath_Xx`  
🎮 *playing Siege of Shrewsbury*  
**[Join call]**

Joining it puts the player into a social scene. They might ostensibly be playing something, watching something or doing nothing at all. The important gameplay is **what happens between the people**.

You don't need to simulate the game they're playing.

You might get:

> **JAMIE:** Where's Connor?
>
> **NIAMH:** Job interview tomorrow. Says he needs an early night.
>
> *Connor joined the call.*
>
> **CONNOR:** Right. One game.
>
> **11:47 PM**
>
> **CONNOR:** Right. One more.

And the player can participate, listen, leave, have somebody pull them into a private conversation, agree to something for tomorrow, hear gossip, get invited somewhere, etc.

That fits Managed Decline extremely well.

---

# It also solves an important problem with your social world

We've been talking about giving the player lots of culturally specific **planned activities** rather than endless mechanical hobbies.

Online hanging out fills a different niche.

It is **low-friction social life**.

Going bowling might require:

- organising four people;
- travelling there;
- paying £18.50;
- discovering somebody misunderstood the booking;
- getting home afterwards.

Joining your mates online requires pressing **Join**.

That difference matters in a game about contemporary Britain. The cost of living remains one of the dominant concerns reported by UK adults, while government youth research specifically identifies lower living costs and access to leisure activities among things young people say would improve their lives.

It gives you a really useful rhythm:

**big physical social experiences**  
↕  
**ordinary evenings online**

The spectacular Saturday night becomes more memorable because Wednesday was four people sitting in voice chat while one of them unsuccessfully tried to fix their printer.

---

# Don't portray online friends as inferior friends

This is probably the most important creative rule.

I would avoid the conventional game logic:

> went outside = healthy  
> played games = sad hermit

That's both boring and increasingly false to people's experience.

An online friend might be one of the **player's closest relationships**.

Someone you've never physically met could:

- know about your family problems;
- remember your birthday;
- sit up talking to you until 3 AM;
- help you through a breakup;
- lend you £20;
- disappear for six months;
- eventually visit;
- become a romance;
- turn out to be completely different face-to-face;
- remain an online-only friend for the entire game.

Recent research into long-term online friendship specifically describes these as durable relationships that span platforms and can carry considerable personal significance. Research on Discord also characterises servers as capable of functioning as **digital “third places”** built around repeated, low-pressure interaction and shared activities.

That's excellent territory for this game because it complicates the definition of **community**.

## And there is a very Managed Decline tension available

Online life can simultaneously be:

**genuinely wonderful**

and

**a slightly horrifying substitute for a society that has made physical social life harder.**

Don't resolve that contradiction for the player.

Consider two evenings.

### Thursday

Your online friends are watching a dreadful fictional 2004 British paranormal programme together. Everyone is taking the piss out of it. Someone falls asleep on voice chat. Another person admits they're frightened about losing their job.

It's warm, intimate and real.

### Saturday

Someone asks:

> “Anyone actually want to do something?”

There is a long pause.

One person can't afford it.

One doesn't want to take two buses.

Someone else says there is nowhere open near them anyway.

Eventually:

> “Minecraft?”

**Everyone joins immediately.**

That's funny, slightly depressing, completely affectionate and much closer to Managed Decline's tone than telling the player that society is collapsing through exposition.

# The online world can expand Britain rather than dilute it

There is one genuine danger: **Discord culture is international.**

If too much of the game occurs inside generic internet culture, Managed Decline could stop feeling specifically British.

I'd solve this partly through the cast.

Perhaps the player's regular online group contains:

- someone from Glasgow;
- someone from Birmingham;
- an old school friend who moved to Bristol;
- somebody in a tiny Welsh town;
- one inexplicably close Dutch person;
- somebody whose actual location nobody knows.

Now the internet allows the game to show **different Britains at once**.

It also lets you make extremely British regional arguments completely naturally.

> “You can't call that a bread roll.”
>
> “I didn't.”
>
> “You literally just called it a bap.”
>
> “It IS a bap.”
>
> “I've muted both of you.”

And an occasional foreign member can make the country's absurdities much funnier simply by asking perfectly reasonable questions.

> **LUKAS:** Why don't you just email the council?
>
> *Five British people start talking simultaneously.*

# Online gaming itself should stay abstract

I would create a small library of **fictional games**, but they exist primarily as social props.

| Game | Social function |
|---|---|
| chaotic competitive shooter | arguments, shouting, unlikely teamwork |
| cosy building game | long personal conversations |
| ancient MMO everyone somehow still plays | nostalgia, old friendships |
| football game | rivalry and banter |
| four-player horror game | screaming and comedy |
| terrible free-to-play game | everyone hates it but keeps playing |
| party/trivia game | larger gatherings |
| deeply bureaucratic strategy game | attracts one very specific NPC |

The player selects:

**Play something together**

and perhaps chooses the game.

Then the game becomes a **short interactive montage/social scene**, not twenty minutes of fake gameplay.

That matches the direction we've established for work, routines and other repeated activities: **play the interesting moments; compress repetition.**

---

# Movie nights could be particularly good

I'd absolutely include:

**Watch something together**

because it gives your fictional British media ecosystem another purpose.

You can make fake:

- terrible reality television;
- prestigious BBC-style dramas;
- bargain-bin horror films;
- documentaries;
- old children's programmes everyone remembers;
- political interviews;
- football;
- Eurovision-like events;
- disastrous live television.

Characters reacting to media tells you a great deal about them without requiring enormous story events.

Someone laughs at completely different things from you.

Someone talks through the entire film.

Someone disappears whenever romance is discussed.

Someone unironically loves dreadful television.

Someone pretends not to care and knows every character's name.

And occasionally everybody actually shuts up because the thing they're watching is excellent.

That's **characterisation through hanging out**, which Managed Decline needs.

# Keep the online cast deliberately small

This is where I would be strict.

Given the existing risk of NPC overload, **do not create realistic servers containing 150 named usernames**.

The player's online social universe should probably contain something like:

**3–6 genuinely recurring online friends**, plus occasional peripheral usernames.

Someone posting in a server doesn't automatically become a Character™.

Most people can remain:

`BirminghamBastard92`  
`MothEnjoyer`  
`Dave`  
`[deleted]`

They provide atmosphere.

Only people who become socially important graduate into the player's meaningful character network.

This also creates a lovely effect where initially:

> **MothEnjoyer:** anyone on tonight

means nothing.

Thirty hours later:

> **MothEnjoyer:** anyone on tonight

means **oh, Rosie is back**.

---

# There should be some online-only incidents

Not quests in the usual sense. Little pieces of life.

A friend messages:

> can't sleep, you about?

Another player joins the call drunk after their office Christmas party.

Someone hasn't appeared online for twelve days.

Two members of the group have clearly fallen out but refuse to explain why.

Someone changes their username after a breakup.

Someone streams their screen while filling in an absurd government form and everybody attempts to help.

An online friend announces they're coming to your city next month.

Someone you've known for years finally turns their camera on.

Your entire group decides at 1 AM that they're going to book a holiday together. Nobody books anything.

Then, occasionally, **they actually do**.

Those transitions from digital nonsense into consequential physical life could be extremely powerful.

## The important design distinction

I wouldn't implement:

> **ONLINE GAMING SYSTEM**  
> Gaming Skill +2  
> Win matches → XP  
> Unlock better PC  
> Increase Gamer Friendship

I would implement:

> **ONLINE SOCIAL SPACE**  
> Games, films and internet culture are things characters do together.

Then it becomes part of the same social simulation as pubs, work, dating, family, hobbies and community instead of another subsystem demanding content.

And because online socialising can happen spontaneously, it complements the planned-out physical activities nicely. A notification at 9:14 PM saying **“Jamie started a call”** creates exactly the sort of small everyday choice Managed Decline can use:

**Join them / continue what you're doing / say you'll be on later / ignore it.**

No quest marker. No punishment. Just life moving around you.

I think that is sufficiently important to contemporary young British life that I'd now consider **some form of online friendship/community a core part of Managed Decline's social model**, rather than an optional piece of flavour.
