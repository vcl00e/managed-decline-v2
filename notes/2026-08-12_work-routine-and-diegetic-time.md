# Work, Routine and Diegetic Time

## Status

**Agreed design direction.**

The foundational rule is:

> **Diegetic calendar underneath, elastic narrative time above it, and direct gameplay only when the player's attention or agency matters.**

A useful implementation test follows from this: if a system requires the player to repeatedly perform routine actions simply to keep ordinary life functioning, it is probably violating the design. Routine should create **context, contrast, obligations, relationships and consequences**, not busywork.

---

## User

How should work or routine be portrayed and experienced in Managed Decline? We've established that we don't want grind, but what is the experience? Should time be diegetic?

## Assistant

Yes — **time should be diegetic**, but *Managed Decline* should strongly avoid becoming a clock-management game.

The best model is:

> **The world has real days, mornings, evenings, work shifts, weekends, appointments and deadlines — but ordinary time is elastic and compresses around anything that is not currently interesting.**

That gives routine meaning without turning it into grind.

## Work should be a life structure, not a job simulator

The player's job should primarily:

- give their week **shape and constraints**;
- put them repeatedly around a particular **social ecosystem**;
- create money, status, obligations, gossip, resentment, opportunities and absurdity;
- provide recurring situations whose meaning changes as the player's life changes.

The actual repetitive labour is mostly assumed.

If the player works in some miserable council-adjacent office, they should **not repeatedly click "process form", "answer email", "make spreadsheet"**.

Instead, a normal workday might effectively become:

> You arrive late because the bus has mysteriously been replaced by a minibus operated by a company called MobilityFuture.
>
> Your colleague mentions the department restructure.
>
> You can get on with your work, investigate why Sandra is crying in Meeting Room 3, disappear for a suspiciously long coffee with Tariq, or use the office printer for something completely unrelated.
>
> Later, your manager asks you something awkward.
>
> The rest of the afternoon passes uneventfully.

The **boring work happened**. The game simply did not force the player to perform it.

That distinction is important.

---

## A normal day should contain a lot of implied life

Do not structure the game as:

**08:00 → 08:15 → 08:30 → 08:45...**

Nor expose something like:

**3 Action Points Remaining**

Both make the underlying machinery too visible.

Instead, the player experiences natural temporal anchors such as:

**Morning → commute → workday → lunch-ish period → after work → evening → late night**

These are not necessarily fixed gameplay turns.

Sometimes:

> **Tuesday morning**
> You go to work.

...and twenty seconds later it is Tuesday evening because nothing important happened.

Another day, **Tuesday afternoon might take forty minutes to play** because everything goes wrong.

That is much closer to how stories represent life.

---

## Diegetic time should be real; simulation time should be elastic

The game knows that:

- today is Wednesday;
- Lucy works Wednesdays;
- the pub quiz is Thursday night;
- payday is Friday;
- the council hearing is next Tuesday;
- someone's birthday is in two weeks;
- winter is approaching;
- the player's landlord expects an answer eventually.

Characters can naturally say:

> "You're still coming Thursday, right?"

or:

> "You've been saying that for three weeks."

That makes the world feel much more substantial than an RPG where everyone permanently waits for the protagonist.

But the player does **not** need to experience every hour between those events.

### Time passes because things happen

Walking across the room does essentially nothing.

Going across London takes meaningful time.

Having lunch with somebody advances the day.

Working a normal shift advances most of the day.

Spending the evening helping somebody consumes the evening.

Going home and playing games until 3am gets you to 3am.

The player should intuitively understand this without calculating it.

---

## Routine should gradually become automated

The player establishes **what kind of person their character normally is** through play.

After repeatedly behaving similarly, the game can effectively understand things such as:

> Goes to work unless there's a reason not to.
> Usually takes the train.
> Buys lunch rather than preparing it.
> Chats with Raj during lunch.
> Does enough work not to attract attention.
> Usually goes straight home afterward.

The game should not need to ask the player to confirm these behaviours every day.

They become the character's **default routine**.

Then gameplay occurs when something interrupts it.

### Monday

> You work. Nothing especially interesting happens.

**Advance.**

### Tuesday

> Raj isn't at his desk.

Now the game pauses.

That is interesting precisely because there was a routine to violate.

Routine therefore becomes a powerful narrative device rather than dead gameplay.

---

## Players should always be able to break their routine

This is essential for agency.

The system might present something like:

> **Wednesday morning**
> You're due at work.

And circumstances make alternatives naturally apparent:

- **Go to work**
- **Call in sick**
- **Meet Hannah before her train leaves**
- **Go and find out why your bank account has been frozen**

The player is not spending an "action point".

They are deciding:

> **What matters to me today?**

Calling in sick once may be completely fine.

Doing it habitually might slowly cause colleagues to cover for the player, a manager to distrust them, somebody to discover why they were actually absent, or perhaps strangely make no difference at all because the department has not had meaningful leadership since 2019.

That is where comedy and simulation reinforce each other.

---

## Work performance should mostly be qualitative

Avoid something like:

> JOB PERFORMANCE: 73/100

The game can track more internally, but the player should perceive performance socially.

They might be:

- regarded as reliable;
- thought of as talented but impossible;
- quietly carrying the department;
- obviously checked out;
- the person everyone gives awful jobs to because they will do them;
- strangely well-liked despite accomplishing very little;
- being protected by their manager;
- one embarrassing incident away from a formal conversation.

Different people can disagree.

A boss might think the player is useless while colleagues think they are the only competent person there.

That is richer than a work XP bar.

---

## Occasionally, actually doing the work should become gameplay

The key is that **mundane activity becomes interesting when context makes it meaningful**.

Normally:

> Finish the quarterly report.

Skip it.

But suppose the player discovers that the report contains evidence of something ridiculous.

Now working on it could involve:

- deciding what to include;
- deciding whom to tell;
- hiding something;
- asking somebody for help;
- manipulating the wording;
- accidentally discovering another problem;
- deciding whether to submit it at all.

The task did not become interesting because spreadsheets are inherently enjoyable.

It became interesting because **the player's intent now intersects with the work**.

This gives a useful rule:

> **Never simulate routine labour merely because the character is employed. Simulate it when the way the player performs it matters.**

---

## Jobs can generate stories very cheaply

A workplace is not primarily a collection of bespoke minigames.

It is a **persistent social/narrative system**:

**people + hierarchy + obligations + recurring spaces + institutional problems + outside events**

A relatively small cast can produce enormous amounts of material because they repeatedly encounter one another.

Hidden event/story systems can also create coordinated effects.

For example, a developing **council funding crisis** might manifest over several weeks as:

- somebody joking about stationery disappearing;
- strange headlines;
- a contractor appearing;
- overtime being frozen;
- an NPC becoming increasingly stressed;
- management denying anything is happening;
- an absurd consultation exercise;
- someone unexpectedly getting promoted;
- a service disappearing;
- eventually a restructuring.

The player might encounter only half of those manifestations.

Work gives those developments somewhere to **land**.

---

## Routine makes change emotionally visible

This may be one of its biggest narrative functions.

Imagine the player has worked somewhere for months.

They know:

> where somebody sits;
> who makes tea;
> who arrives late;
> what everyone complains about;
> where they go at lunch.

Then one morning:

> **Sandra's desk is empty.**

That means something.

Similarly, if the player's life gradually improves or deteriorates, the same commute can feel completely different.

Early game:

> commuting to a crap job because rent is due.

Later:

> commuting there despite no longer really needing the money because you care about somebody there.

Or:

> turning up in clothing that suddenly makes everybody realise you've somehow become wealthy.

Or eventually:

> walking past the bus stop you used every morning.

This connects strongly to the wealth-transition design. **Keeping ordinary routines around for a long time gives later changes something to contrast against.**

Without normality, abnormality has much less impact.

---

## The calendar should be important but forgiving

There should be real appointments and opportunities:

> Friday night — someone's party
> Monday — work presentation
> sometime this week — promised to help somebody move
> end of month — rent
> Saturday — football / protest / disastrous family lunch / whatever

But *Managed Decline* should not behave like *Persona* where the player starts optimising a calendar spreadsheet.

Do not make every decision mutually exclusive.

Some things should legitimately collide because that is interesting:

> Mum's birthday dinner is the same night somebody important asks you to come somewhere.

Excellent.

But most of the game should not punish players because they used Tuesday afternoon "incorrectly."

The narrative controller should actively avoid creating a huge backlog of simultaneous obligations.

This fits the wider pacing rule: **the game should regulate how much is demanding the player's attention.**

---

## Missing things should create stories, not delete content

Traditional timed quests can produce anxiety:

> **YOU FAILED TO ATTEND SARAH'S EVENT. QUEST FAILED.**

The preferred philosophy is:

> **The world continued without you.**

Sarah went.

Something happened.

She remembers that the player did not come.

Maybe somebody else accompanied her.

Maybe it was completely fine.

Maybe next time they meet:

> "Oh, *now* you've got time."

And the relationship continues from there.

Missing events should therefore generate **different content**, rather than merely losing content.

There can be rare genuinely irreversible moments, but they should feel narratively justified.

---

## The player should be able to consciously let time pass

This is necessary because otherwise simulation becomes annoying.

At home, something like:

> **Nothing I particularly want to do.**

could naturally allow the player to move forward.

Or interacting with the bed, sofa or calendar might offer context-sensitive choices such as:

- Stay in tonight
- Turn in early
- Get through the working week
- Wait until Saturday

The game then compresses routine appropriately and stops if something interesting happens.

For example:

> **Thursday, 12:17**
> Tariq messages you.

The fast-forward ends because the player now has a meaningful decision.

This is essentially **narrative fast-forward rather than simulation fast-forward**.

---

## Three temporal speeds

These should not necessarily appear as explicit modes; they describe how the game behaves.

### 1. Lived time

Moment-to-moment exploration, conversations and decisions.

> You're in the pub with someone.
> Something important is happening.

Minutes are experienced almost continuously.

### 2. Routine time

Large sections compress.

> Worked Wednesday. Came home. Made pasta. Watched something awful on Channel 4.

Hours disappear in seconds.

### 3. Narrative time

When nothing relevant is happening:

> **Three fairly uneventful weeks pass.**

Characters, jobs, finances, relationships and world events still update through controlled simulation.

Then play resumes when something changes.

This makes it possible to tell a story spanning **months or possibly years** without requiring hundreds of hours of repetitive gameplay.

---

## What work should feel like

The intended fantasy is not:

> **"I am efficiently completing a job."**

It is closer to:

> **"I have a life, and work is this enormous recurring social/institutional thing I somehow have to coexist with."**

Some mornings the player is interested in what is happening there.

Some mornings they barely care.

Sometimes they are desperate not to get fired.

Sometimes the job becomes unexpectedly meaningful.

Sometimes coworkers become important friends.

Sometimes there is an enormous institutional crisis.

Sometimes absolutely nothing happens and they go home.

And occasionally the player should think:

> "I cannot believe I'm going into work after what happened last night."

That feeling requires diegetic routine.

---

# Core Design Principle

> **Simulate commitments, not chores.**

The player's life contains real recurring commitments:

**work, sleep, relationships, travel, money, appointments, personal projects and eventually whatever goals they choose for themselves.**

The game continuously compresses their ordinary fulfilment.

It slows down whenever:

- something disrupts the routine;
- a decision has meaningful consequences;
- an interesting social interaction occurs;
- the player deliberately wants to intervene;
- the current activity advances one of the player's own goals;
- or the narrative controller knows something worth experiencing is developing.

This should make *Managed Decline* feel like someone **living through a British life**, rather than playing a time-management simulator with a British skin.

Architecturally, this implies a strong separation between:

1. **diegetic calendar/world simulation underneath**;
2. **narrative time-compression layer above it**;
3. **moment-to-moment gameplay only where player attention is valuable**.

This should be treated as a foundational design rule for the prototype.
