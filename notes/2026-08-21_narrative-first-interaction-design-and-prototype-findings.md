That is not the correct standard for *Managed Decline*. It would systematically select for mechanics capable of competing with the narrative, then force the narrative to justify their presence. The interaction may derive most of its value from context. The strongest direct precedent is *Mutazione*. The correct direction is not:

> narrative wrapped around a strong independent toy

It is:

> ## **A strong interactive ensemble narrative supported by a coherent language of presence, expression, interpretation, commitment, physical ritual and persistent residue.**

# What professional narrative-led games actually do

The research does not identify one universal solution. Professional teams use several recurring approaches, chosen according to the kind of story and agency they want.

## 1. They establish a creative hierarchy

The most important decision is not “what mechanics should we add?” It is:

> **What is the game fundamentally about, and what must remain subordinate to that?**

*Mutazione* is unusually transparent about this. Once its team chose the community and characters as the priority, they restructured and cut mechanics accordingly. The Forest Paths Method similarly starts from the dramatic question and the game’s pillars, then combines narrative and design elements into patterns that serve those foundations. ([gutefabrik.com](https://gutefabrik.com/narrative-design-sliding-doors-gardens/))

This means *Managed Decline* needs a written hierarchy before evaluating any new mechanism:

1. Characters, relationships and authored narrative.
2. The experience of inhabiting a particular life in contemporary Britain.
3. Player expression and consequential participation.
4. Physical, temporal and material embodiment.
5. Systems that regulate and personalise the experience.
6. Everything else.

A mechanism that improves level-four materiality while damaging level-one character focus fails, even if it is enjoyable in isolation.

---

## 2. They define what the player is doing **with the story**

Night School’s *Oxenfree* development repeatedly returned to two questions:

> Why is the player playing this?  
> What is the player doing while the developers have them?

For *Oxenfree*, the “why” was defining Alex’s story, while its communication and exploration mechanics made interacting with that story personal and novel. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024270/Building-Game-Mechanics-to-Elevate))

That is more useful for *Managed Decline* than asking for a conventional core toy.

The design question becomes:

> **What cognitive, emotional and expressive work is the player performing while the narrative unfolds?**

Possible answers include:

- deciding whom to attend to;
- interpreting incomplete social evidence;
- choosing how to present themselves;
- deciding what to say or leave unsaid;
- committing their time to people;
- determining whether to become involved;
- physically positioning themselves within a social situation;
- deciding which objects, messages and memories to keep or expose;
- living through consequences.

Those activities can constitute play even without combat, puzzles or fail states. *Firewatch* was explicitly designed around the possibility that the story itself could be gameplay, using real-time responsiveness, exploration and player-centric interaction without traditional mechanical failure. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026087/Interactive-Story-Without-Challenge-Mechanics))

---

## 3. They use **small interactions with high contextual meaning**

The professional answer is often not a deep reusable economy. It can be a small, low-friction interaction that embodies one part of a story.

*What Remains of Edith Finch* repeatedly introduces new control schemes intended to capture the feel of a unique action. Each had to be learned and effectively mastered almost immediately, without explicit tutorials, so it would not distract from the narrative. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024948/Foolproofing-the-Controls-for-What))

*Fragments of Him* was designed around keeping the player involved in the story through many small interaction choices. Its developers also found that removing interactions could sometimes improve immersion. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023839/Narrative-Experience-First-Interaction-Design))

This provides an important correction:

> **More interaction is not automatically more agency, more embodiment or a better narrative game.**

A strong narrative game may need:

- a small recurring interaction vocabulary;
- occasional bespoke interactions for landmark scenes;
- and many moments where the player is deliberately allowed simply to listen, observe or choose not to act.

---

## 4. They make objects and environments carry narrative meaning

*Unpacking* tells a life story through the repeated physical handling and placement of household objects. The mechanic is simple, but the items reveal relationships, heartbreak, careers and changing domestic circumstances. The player is not completing a detached puzzle before receiving a story scene; handling the objects **is how the story becomes understood**. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027934/Independent-Games-Summit-Unpacking-a))

Environmental-storytelling practice similarly treats the world as a space from which the player actively pulls meaning. Props, composition, lighting, scripted events and world reactions invite the player to infer what happened. *Tacoma* went further by shaping levels around nonlinear, self-directed story discovery. ([gdcvault.com](https://gdcvault.com/play/1012647/What-Happened-Here-Environmental))

For *Managed Decline*, physicality therefore should not principally mean:

- carrying equipment;
- arranging event logistics;
- operating machines;
- solving spatial optimisation problems.

It should mean:

- seeing who is where;
- noticing who is absent;
- deciding whether to approach;
- choosing where to stand or sit;
- handling socially meaningful objects;
- observing changes to familiar places;
- entering private versus public space;
- deciding whether to keep, give, expose or destroy something.

The physical world becomes a **social and autobiographical stage**, not a logistics sandbox.

---

## 5. They turn interpretation itself into a mechanism

In *Heaven’s Vault*, translation is narratively significant. The player’s interpretation affects what the protagonist believes about discoveries, and wrong translations can send the player down a different conceptual path rather than merely producing a puzzle failure. ([inklestudios.com](https://www.inklestudios.com/heavensvault/))

This is highly relevant to *Managed Decline*, because “reading Britain” is already one of the project’s strongest ideas. The hidden situation network is designed to express one underlying reality through changed routines, behaviour, environmental states, official language, news and rumours.

The player can therefore have real gameplay competence in:

- identifying contradictions;
- forming provisional explanations;
- deciding whom to believe;
- recognising institutional patterns;
- noticing what someone is avoiding;
- understanding what a public statement does and does not say;
- deciding which version of events to repeat.

This is not conventional detective puzzle-solving. The player need not eventually select the one correct answer. Their interpretation can determine:

- what they ask;
- whom they approach;
- what they disclose;
- what risks they take;
- how other people understand their behaviour.

That turns narrative comprehension into participation.

---

## 6. They use light systems to generate momentum, not dominate the experience

*80 Days* began as a choice narrative with health and money, but evolved into what its developers described as a board game that narrates itself. Its map, economy, art and other elements work together to create narrative momentum. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1021666/80-DAYS-Post-mortem-Letting))

The relevant lesson is not that *Managed Decline* needs a board-game economy. It is that a small number of comprehensible pressures can:

- force prioritisation;
- turn decisions into commitments;
- generate anticipation;
- create different routes through authored material.

For *Managed Decline*, those pressures should remain mostly diegetic:

- time;
- money;
- promises;
- social expectations;
- access;
- information;
- where the player is physically present.

The accepted work-and-routine design already provides the correct foundation: real diegetic time underneath, elastic compression above it, and direct play only when attention or agency matters.

---

## 7. They do not confuse agency with maximum branching

Players tend to perceive greater agency when they can foresee that choices will produce meaningfully different **situational states**, not merely differently worded responses. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12716))

But not every choice should be an agonising dilemma. Choice-poetics research has demonstrated that choices can intentionally have different functions: relaxed choices, obvious choices and dilemmas produce different experiential effects. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12791))

*The Stanley Parable* similarly demonstrates that meaningful expressive choices can exist without overcoming an obstacle, while *Mutazione* deliberately pursued “multiple middles” rather than making agency depend on radically different endings. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020637/The-Stanley-Parable-A-Negotiation))

This is important for *Managed Decline*. The player can meaningfully choose:

- who to spend an ordinary evening with;
- whether to answer;
- whether to make a joke;
- whether to keep sitting beside someone;
- whether to bring a particular person;
- whether to wear something conspicuous;
- whether to tell the public or private version;
- whether to leave early.

These choices do not need to branch the entire campaign. They can change:

- the emotional meaning of a scene;
- what somebody remembers;
- who witnesses something;
- what information travels;
- how the player’s life feels.

---

## 8. Structure and agency are not opposites

Recent interactive-narrative research found a strong relationship between participants’ perceptions of narrative structure and agency. It characterised bad experiences partly as failures of improvisational partnership between the player and the story manager, rather than simply “too much authorial control.” ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/36827))

Other experience-management research has found that preserving player actions while controlling or selecting plausible NPC actions can increase perceived agency and NPC believability. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/5229))

The implication is:

> **Managed Decline does not need to surrender narrative authority in order to respect the player.**

It needs to “accept the offer”:

- remember what the player did;
- let characters interpret it plausibly;
- reincorporate it where useful;
- preserve consequences;
- avoid silently negating it.

But there is also an important warning. Research on reincorporation found that producing more structurally unified stories did not automatically improve players’ reported experience of story quality or agency. A technically coherent narrative machine is not sufficient. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12468))

That describes the current project risk precisely: the architecture can become more coherent without the experience becoming better.

---

## 9. The systems themselves inevitably make a thematic argument

The creator of *The Novelist* tried to remove authorial bias from the narrative, then discovered that the rules governing relationships had simply become the new site of authorship: the system itself was communicating the game’s values. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020428/The-System-Is-the-Message))

Consequently, a *Managed Decline* mechanic is not neutral.

For example:

- If every relationship becomes leverage, the game says people are tools.
- If every missed event causes punishment, the game says life is obligation.
- If every institution is exploitable once understood, the game says competence defeats politics.
- If every social problem can be solved by arranging enough resources, the game says society is an optimisation problem.
- If the player is rewarded mainly for intervention, the game devalues ordinary presence and companionship.

That is why the previous project-driven logistics proposal felt wrong. It silently changed the game’s thesis from:

> What kind of life can people make together in this society?

to:

> How effectively can the player mobilise social and material resources?

Those are not the same game.

# The corrected model: narrative is the game; interaction is its handling language

The repository’s current diagnosis remains valid: *Managed Decline* has sophisticated consequence architecture, but its moment-to-moment interaction is unproven and could still become walking, reading, selecting and receiving invisible state changes.

The correction is not to import a mechanically dominant toy.

The correction is to design a **Narrative Support Grammar**.

## Five jobs of narrative-supporting interaction

Every recurring mechanic should perform at least one of these jobs.

| Job | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| **Embodiment** | The story feeling as though it happens only in text boxes and databases |
| **Expression** | The protagonist feeling like a spectator who only chooses outcomes |
| **Comprehension** | Complex world state becoming obscure or arbitrary |
| **Commitment** | Choices becoming weightless because nothing has been risked or promised |
| **Residue** | Consequences feeling abstract because nothing visible or reusable remains |

A sixth system, **rhythm**, controls when these interactions occur and when the player is simply allowed to inhabit the world.

The resulting core loop is not:

```text
acquire tools
→ solve problem
→ receive upgrades
```

It is:

```text
BE PRESENT
        ↓
NOTICE AND INTERPRET
        ↓
EXPRESS WHO YOU ARE
        ↓
COMMIT, WITHDRAW OR LET THE MOMENT PASS
        ↓
PERFORM A SMALL MEANINGFUL ACT
        ↓
SEE PEOPLE AND PLACES RESPOND
        ↓
LIVE WITH THE RESIDUE
        ↓
ENTER THE NEXT PART OF YOUR LIFE
```

That is still a game loop. It is simply a narrative-first one.

# A professional methodology for finding the right solutions

Professional quality cannot be guaranteed by adopting a list of mechanics from successful games. Postmortems show useful reasoning, not transferable recipes. The final quality must be established through narrative prototyping and user research.

GDC guidance on narrative prototyping explicitly recommends testing interactive story structure early and cheaply, before code, while Microsoft’s narrative-usability work was developed to detect comprehension failures, ineffective twists and confused character perception early enough to iterate. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020509/Paper-Tales-A-Guide-to))

I recommend the following methodology.

## Stage 1 — Establish the Creative Constitution

Before generating any mechanism, define five non-negotiable statements.

### The primary fantasy

For *Managed Decline*:

> **Build and inhabit a particular young-adult life in contemporary Britain, becoming entangled with memorable people and experiencing how ordinary choices accumulate into relationships, stories and a personal social world.**

### The dramatic question

A useful candidate:

> **What makes a life worth building in a society that frequently frustrates, divides and diminishes the people living in it?**

This question is broad enough to contain:

- love;
- friendship;
- ambition;
- money;
- status;
- community;
- escape;
- creative life;
- political involvement;
- selfishness;
- kindness;
- absurdity.

### The player’s role

> The player is a resident and participant, not a saviour, manager, detective assigned to solve everything or omnipotent social engineer.

### The experiential priorities

In order:

1. Character attachment and authored dramatic quality.
2. Personal ownership of the protagonist’s life.
3. A coherent, socially persistent world.
4. British comedy, texture and satire.
5. Physical and material embodiment.
6. Replay variation.
7. Strategic or mechanical depth.

### The prohibited transformations

A proposed system fails if it turns the game primarily into:

- resource logistics;
- project management;
- social combat;
- calendar optimisation;
- detective deduction;
- life-maintenance simulation;
- a collection treadmill;
- an unrestricted procedural story generator.

This constitution becomes the authority for cuts.

---

## Stage 2 — Define the player’s **narrative labour**

Take each important type of scene and ask:

> What should the player be mentally or emotionally doing here?

Use precise verbs.

| Narrative situation | Desired player labour |
|---|---|
| Meeting an attractive or intriguing character | Read, test, express, risk |
| Friend discloses something | Attend, interpret, reassure, avoid or challenge |
| Public institutional scene | Observe framing, choose alignment, decide public/private conduct |
| Group event | Navigate attention, notice relationships, choose participation |
| Mystery or contradiction | Compare, suspect, ask, revise interpretation |
| Ordinary companionship | Inhabit, affirm, relax, build familiarity |
| Commitment | Anticipate, prioritise, fulfil, cancel or disappoint |
| Aftermath | Recognise causality, reflect, repair or distance |
| Familiar place changes | Notice loss or opportunity, decide whether it matters |

This stage prevents the design from reaching immediately for “a mechanic.” First determine the intended human activity.

---

## Stage 3 — Perform an Abstraction-Gap Audit

For every scene or system, identify where the narrative currently risks becoming abstract.

Use these categories:

### Told but not witnessed

> “The event was a disaster.”

The player never attended it.

### Chosen but not performed

> “Comfort Maya.”

The game resolves the action through one line and a hidden relationship update.

### Changed but not visible

> The hall is supposedly thriving, but looks and behaves exactly the same.

### Earned but not possessed

> The player gained trust or belonging, but has no new access, ritual, object or behaviour demonstrating it.

### Important but not remembered

> A major scene has no later callback, object, message, environmental trace or altered routine.

### Physical but meaningless

> The player walks somewhere or clicks objects, but those actions do not affect attention, interpretation, expression or consequence.

Every proposed interaction should address a documented abstraction gap.

Do not add mechanics to a scene that has no gap.

---

## Stage 4 — Generate candidates from seven support families

For each gap, generate alternatives from several different families rather than converging immediately.

| Family | Candidate forms |
|---|---|
| **Presence** | approach, remain, leave, follow, accompany, wait, sit, stand with someone |
| **Expression** | speak, interrupt, joke, withhold, disclose, lie, perform an action instead of talking |
| **Commitment** | accept, tentatively agree, refuse, cancel, arrive late, renegotiate, bring someone |
| **Interpretation** | compare accounts, choose what to ask, form a provisional view, decide what evidence means |
| **Material ritual** | bring, give, keep, return, delete, forward, wear, pack, place, photograph |
| **Residue** | object, photograph, changed place, new routine, private access, message history, public trace |
| **Rhythm** | linger, fast-forward, montage, afterglow, interruption, silence |

Then ask whether the correct solution is actually:

> **No additional interaction.**

That option must always remain available. The *Fragments of Him* experience is a professional reminder that removing an interaction can strengthen immersion. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023839/Narrative-Experience-First-Interaction-Design))

---

## Stage 5 — Create a Narrative Mechanic Contract

No proposed interaction enters production without a compact contract.

```yaml
interaction:
  name: keep_or_return_jacket

  dramatic_purpose:
    make_the_relationship_change_material

  player_experience:
    decide_whether_to_accept_intimacy_or_restore_distance

  verb:
    keep | return | wear

  narrative_input:
    jacket_was_lent_after_bad_night

  immediate_feedback:
    object_appears_at_home
    lender_reacts

  future_use:
    can_wear_it
    character_may_notice
    breakup_or_reconciliation_callback

  repetition_class:
    landmark

  learning_burden:
    immediate

  motivation_lanes:
    story
    roleplay
    expression
    collection

  anti_goals:
    no_clothing_stats
    no_affection_reward_display
    no_inventory_management

  kill_condition:
    testers_treat_it_as_cosmetic_loot
```

This forces narrative, systems, UX and production to agree on why the interaction exists.

---

## Stage 6 — Apply the hard quality gate

A candidate should pass every relevant requirement below.

### Narrative necessity

Removing the interaction would lose a specific emotional, causal, expressive or thematic quality.

### Semantic clarity

The player understands what they are doing in ordinary human terms.

### Foreseeable distinction

The player can anticipate how the alternatives differ in meaning, even if the precise outcome remains uncertain. This follows the evidence that foreseeably different situational outcomes support agency. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12716))

### Believability

The options and reactions make sense within the current character, relationship and situation. Visible system manipulation can undermine believability. ([ojs.aaai.org](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12907))

### Consequence

The action changes at least one of:

- interpretation;
- relationship behaviour;
- information flow;
- commitment;
- access;
- physical state;
- future scene context;
- persistent memory.

### Visible shadow

The consequence becomes inferable without requiring an exposed numerical meter.

### Narrative proportionality

A five-second emotional beat does not receive a two-minute interface.

### Low learning burden

A recurring interaction is highly consistent. A bespoke landmark interaction can be understood almost immediately, following the *Edith Finch* principle. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024948/Foolproofing-the-Controls-for-What))

### No optimisation capture

The mechanic does not produce an obviously superior strategy that overrides role-playing.

### No maintenance debt

Using it once does not commit the player to repetitive upkeep unless that obligation is itself the dramatic subject.

### Tonal consistency

The tactile feel, timing, interface and consequences support the scene’s emotional register.

### Production proportionality

Either:

- the interaction is reusable across many scenes;
- or the scene is important enough to justify a bespoke implementation.

### Accessibility

The narrative meaning does not depend solely on dexterity, speed, hearing, colour distinction or inaccessible physical input.

### Motivational contribution

The interaction serves at least one intended player motivation and does not distort the game toward an unintended audience.

---

## Stage 7 — Prototype comparatively, not impressionistically

For the same piece of authored narrative, build three deliberately different versions.

### A. Narrative-only baseline

- movement;
- ordinary dialogue choices;
- existing presentation.

### B. Narrative-support version

- small presence, expression, material or interpretive interaction;
- visible residue.

### C. Mechanically stronger version

- more explicit system;
- greater challenge, optimisation or manipulation.

Use the same writing, characters and major outcome so the interaction is the principal variable.

The question is not:

> “Which version is most game-like?”

It is:

> **Which version produces the strongest combined result across comprehension, emotional engagement, agency, character attachment, memory and interaction burden?**

If the baseline wins, do not add a mechanic.

If B wins, the support mechanism earns further development.

If C wins without shifting the perceived identity of the game, reconsider the hierarchy—but do not assume it will.

---

## Stage 8 — Conduct narrative-usability testing

Do not ask only:

> “Was that fun?”

Use observation, retelling and targeted questions.

### Causal comprehension

- What happened?
- Why did it happen?
- Which things were caused by you?
- Which things would have happened anyway?

### Character understanding

- What does this person want?
- Why did they react that way?
- How has your view of them changed?

### Player intention

- What were you trying to communicate or accomplish?
- What did you think each available action meant?
- Did the game understand the way you tried to behave?

### Agency

- Which part felt most like your decision?
- What alternative did you believe was possible?
- Did any outcome feel forced or secretly corrected?

### Embodiment

- Which place, object or physical action do you remember?
- Did you feel present in the event or as though you were operating a dialogue interface?

### Residue

- What changed?
- What do you expect to see next time?
- What do you think the game will remember?

### Interaction burden

- When did you want the game to stop asking for input?
- Which action felt like administration?
- Which action interrupted the emotional moment?

Microsoft’s narrative-usability approach similarly focuses on testing comprehension, character perception and narrative structure early rather than relying only on general enjoyment reports. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020031/Using-User-Research-to-Improve))

---

## Stage 9 — Validate the player-motivation portfolio

Do not try to make the game satisfy every possible player.

Quantic Foundry’s work demonstrates that game motivations are multidimensional and that the presence of a feature does not itself prove the game attracts the corresponding motivation. Their research explicitly links motivations to mechanics and audiences rather than treating “gamers” as homogeneous. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025742/a-deep-dive-into-the))

For *Managed Decline*, I would define the intended portfolio as follows.

### Primary

- story and character attachment;
- relatedness;
- fantasy/identity;
- discovery and interpretation;
- expressive role-playing.

### Secondary

- aesthetic inhabitation;
- completion and autobiographical collection;
- light strategy around time and commitments;
- creative mischief;
- replay comparison.

### Not required

- high dexterity challenge;
- combat mastery;
- power accumulation;
- economic optimisation;
- competitive domination.

Research grounded in self-determination theory also suggests that autonomy, competence and relatedness can independently support enjoyment. Competence does not have to mean combat expertise; it can mean becoming better at reading people, predicting social consequences and navigating the game’s expressive language. ([pure.ewha.ac.kr](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-/))

A major scenario should generally support several intended motivations, but it does not need to satisfy all of them simultaneously.

# Applying the methodology to Managed Decline

## The corrected core fantasy

I would formally define it as:

> ## **Inhabit a particular young-adult life in contemporary Britain. Decide who and what you make time for, how you behave when people and institutions collide, and see those choices accumulate into relationships, places, memories and a life that could only belong to your playthrough.**

This is stronger than:

> solve Britain through social systems

and more active than:

> watch stories happen in a nice town.

The player is not primarily optimising outcomes. They are **authoring their manner of participation**.

---

# The Managed Decline Narrative Support Stack

The professional-quality solution is not one dominant toy. It is a coherent stack of complementary interactions.

## 1. Presence: being somewhere should be a choice

This should be the foundational physical interaction.

The player can:

- approach;
- remain at a distance;
- join a group;
- sit beside someone;
- follow someone who leaves;
- accompany someone home;
- wait;
- enter a private space;
- stay when leaving would be easier;
- leave before the scene has fully resolved.

Presence affects:

- what the player witnesses;
- who can speak to them;
- who hears them;
- what information they receive;
- who believes they supported or avoided;
- what later memories exist.

This gives walking a narrative purpose without turning the diorama into a mechanical obstacle course.

### Example

At a protest outside the council:

- stand with Tabitha;
- stand with a friend working inside;
- remain across the road;
- enter the building;
- leave before the cameras arrive;
- stay after everyone else goes.

These are not dialogue branches. They are **social positions**.

The game can later remember:

> You stood with her publicly.

That is materially and politically different from privately telling her you agreed.

---

## 2. Conversation should happen in a living space

Not every conversation should freeze the player into a VN screen.

Use three presentation levels.

### Ambient conversation

- speech bubbles;
- overheard lines;
- no forced response;
- player may approach or continue walking.

### Live interaction

- player remains spatially present;
- characters move;
- the player may respond, remain silent or leave;
- limited interruption and timing;
- environmental events continue.

### Focused VN scene

Reserved for:

- intimate revelations;
- major confrontations;
- comic set pieces requiring precise timing;
- complex negotiations;
- signature character episodes.

*Oxenfree* and *Firewatch* both use communication as a continuous activity intertwined with exploration rather than treating every exchange as an isolated dialogue tree. *Firewatch* also developed an interruptible conversation system capable of managing conversations that stop and restart around play. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024270/Building-Game-Mechanics-to-Elevate))

The VN presentation remains important. It simply stops carrying every interaction.

---

## 3. Expression: choices define conduct, not success

The canonical response vocabulary should distinguish forms of participation.

For example:

- ask;
- challenge;
- reassure;
- joke;
- disclose;
- conceal;
- redirect;
- refuse;
- promise;
- say nothing;
- perform a physical action;
- leave.

Choice labels should express intention:

> Try to reassure her.

Not guaranteed result:

> Calm her down.

The player should often be able to act instead of speaking:

- hand over the letter;
- put the phone face down;
- move closer;
- leave the object on the table;
- take a photograph;
- open the door;
- sit beside them;
- walk away.

This makes the protagonist feel embodied and permits players who do not want to choose between several speeches to express themselves differently.

---

## 4. Commitment: time becomes narrative weight

The most important “resource” should remain concrete commitment.

The player can:

- accept;
- agree tentatively;
- refuse;
- cancel;
- renegotiate;
- arrive late;
- fail to answer;
- bring somebody else;
- fulfil the promise differently from how the other person expected.

The calendar should show actual expectations:

> Friday, 19:30 — Maya’s radio night  
> Saturday afternoon — said you would help Mum  
> Monday — work meeting

It should not expose:

> Three evening action points remaining.

The player’s current activity always wins. The game never teleports them into a commitment. It gives enough warning that absence is knowingly chosen, then lets the world continue.

This creates:

- anticipation;
- opportunity cost;
- believable pressure;
- different life trajectories.

It also makes relationships concrete. A commitment is not “relationship XP.” It is someone actually expecting you.

---

## 5. Interpretation: let the player construct a view of events

The phone, environment and characters already produce multiple accounts of the same hidden situation.

The player should be able to use this information through a lightweight interpretive grammar.

For example:

```text
Official email:
“No service users will experience an immediate change.”

Locked building:
All bookings cancelled.

Maya:
“They told us the hall isn't closing.”

Caretaker:
“They took the office computers yesterday.”
```

The player may then:

- ask why the accounts conflict;
- repeat the official line;
- tell Maya they think closure is already decided;
- accuse the council prematurely;
- investigate before speaking;
- use ambiguity strategically;
- decide it is none of their concern.

There should not normally be:

> Select the correct solution:  
> A. Closure is confirmed  
> B. Temporary disruption  
> C. Developer conspiracy

The game records what the player has seen and what they have publicly claimed. It need not record one canonical “player theory.”

Incorrect or partial interpretation should produce another coherent narrative, as it does in *Heaven’s Vault*, rather than a puzzle failure. ([inklestudios.com](https://www.inklestudios.com/heavensvault/))

---

## 6. Material narrative rituals

This is the main solution to the “abstract and meaningless” problem.

A **material narrative ritual** is a short physical action whose significance comes from the relationship or situation.

Examples:

- bring someone something they asked for;
- return or keep borrowed clothing;
- choose a gift;
- hand over evidence;
- delete or save a message;
- forward a screenshot;
- choose which photograph to post;
- pack belongings after a breakup;
- place a keepsake at home;
- sign, amend or refuse to sign something;
- wear an item associated with another character;
- give someone a key;
- choose where people sit;
- leave a note;
- carry someone’s bag on the way home;
- cook or order something for a specific person;
- decide whether to display a public poster in a private home.

These should take seconds, not become inventory or crafting systems.

Their value comes from four properties:

1. **The action is physically legible.**
2. **The object or arrangement means something in context.**
3. **A person or place responds.**
4. **Some residue may remain.**

This draws on the material storytelling strength of *Unpacking* without copying its full puzzle structure. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027934/Independent-Games-Summit-Unpacking-a))

---

## 7. Landmark micro-mechanics

Each signature scenario may justify one bespoke tactile interaction.

The interaction should:

- embody the scenario’s central tension;
- be learned immediately;
- avoid dexterity failure;
- last only as long as it remains meaningful;
- leave behind ordinary narrative state.

This is the *Edith Finch* model applied to social narrative rather than death vignettes. ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024948/Foolproofing-the-Controls-for-What))

### Tabitha’s educational programme

The player physically navigates the old institutional training program.

It repeatedly presents:

> What should you do if Tabitha expresses a concerning view?

The interface reduces her into increasingly inadequate options.

Depending on the player’s circumstances, they may:

- complete it sincerely;
- deliberately produce the “wrong” result;
- show it privately to Tabitha;
- screenshot a particular screen;
- post a cropped version;
- give it to a journalist;
- refuse to finish it;
- later encounter a revised version influenced by the controversy.

The mechanic is not an arbitrary minigame. It **embodies the exact thematic violence of an institution converting a person into a decision tree**.

### A breakup or move

The player packs a small number of meaningful objects.

Each object quietly asks:

- mine;
- theirs;
- shared;
- leave behind;
- take despite ambiguity.

No score. No packing puzzle. The final room and later conversations reflect the decisions.

### A public scandal

The player is given several short clips or photographs and chooses:

- which one to post;
- whether to include context;
- whom to send it to;
- whether to wait.

The interaction embodies the transformation of lived reality into public narrative.

### A council complaint

The player decides which pieces of evidence to attach and which parts of their own angry response to remove.

The result affects:

- how the institution categorises the complaint;
- who receives it;
- how the player appears;
- what becomes part of the record.

Again, no “bureaucracy skill” is necessary.

# The physical world: a narrative stage, evidence field and memory surface

The diorama should not become an operational board game.

Each important location should perform five narrative functions.

## Social geography

The player can understand:

- who belongs here;
- who is out of place;
- who is together;
- who is avoiding whom;
- what is public;
- what is private.

## Evidence

The environment can reveal:

- absence;
- deterioration;
- investment;
- changed ownership;
- institutional presence;
- social exclusion;
- preparation for something not yet announced.

## Sequence

The player chooses what to encounter first.

Talking to one person before witnessing another event may change what the player understands, without requiring a formal branch selector.

## Ritual

Places support repeated acts:

- usual seat;
- walking route;
- meeting point;
- after-work stop;
- private corner;
- somebody’s front door.

Routine gives disruption meaning.

## Residue

The place visibly remembers:

- a poster;
- repaired damage;
- an empty desk;
- removed furniture;
- photographs;
- a closed shop;
- new ownership;
- recurring people;
- a plant or object placed there;
- graffiti or notices;
- altered social use.

The accepted affordance-progression design already treats places as moving from merely known to socially meaningful and eventually belonging within the player’s life.

The correction is that access should be emotionally and materially demonstrated, not simply tracked as a hidden graph.

# Tangible rewards for a narrative-first game

“Tangible” does not need to mean weapons, tools or stat upgrades.

A reward is sufficiently tangible when it is:

- **perceptible**;
- **persistent**;
- **personal**;
- and either **reusable or capable of being remembered later**.

## 1. Material traces

- photographs;
- tickets;
- letters;
- borrowed clothing;
- gifts;
- keys;
- books;
- recordings;
- posters;
- ridiculous institutional certificates;
- objects made or acquired during scenes.

## 2. Spatial belonging

- somebody lets the player into their home;
- a staff member no longer challenges them;
- a usual seat exists;
- they can enter through the back;
- a place remains open later for them;
- people expect them there.

## 3. Social rituals

- weekly call;
- shared lunch;
- recurring night out;
- private joke;
- habitual greeting;
- somebody automatically orders for them;
- an online group leaves a space open.

## 4. Communication privilege

- late-night calls;
- private group chat;
- access to a second account;
- unsent drafts shown in confidence;
- somebody replying immediately;
- being consulted before public action.

## 5. Public traces

- a photograph circulating;
- a news item;
- a council record;
- a poster;
- a video;
- a quote attributed to the player;
- a social reputation grounded in something specific.

## 6. Changed environments

- repaired hall;
- closed café;
- new business;
- altered workplace;
- transformed home;
- moved memorial;
- recurring community event.

## 7. Autobiographical accumulation

The phone, home and town together become the player’s save history:

> not a completed-quest list, but evidence of the life they built.

A useful production rule is:

> **Do not persist an object or state unless it changes future access, expresses identity, anchors a memory or alters the visible world.**

That keeps residue meaningful rather than creating collectible clutter.

# Different players extracting different pleasures

The game does not need several separate core fantasies. It needs several **entry points into the same fantasy**.

Consider a major social event.

| Player orientation | What they extract |
|---|---|
| **Character player** | Intimacy, conflict, revelation and attachment |
| **Role-player** | Expressing what kind of protagonist they have become |
| **Discovery player** | Noticing connections, contradictions and private behaviour |
| **Aesthetic inhabitant** | Diorama, music, atmosphere and the pleasure of being there |
| **Light strategist** | Managing promises, timing and information without numerical optimisation |
| **Completionist/collector** | Photographs, objects, callbacks, optional scenes and social history |
| **Comedy player** | Choosing mischief, inappropriate timing and socially risky behaviour |
| **Replay player** | Seeing how different relationships and prior histories change the same landmark |

These do not require different games.

### Example: a rooftop launch attended by Tabitha

The character player is interested in Tabitha’s private/public conflict.

The role-player chooses whether to arrive with her, avoid being photographed with her or publicly support her.

The discovery player notices who organised the event and why a supposedly unrelated councillor is present.

The aesthetic player enjoys the space, music and social atmosphere.

The light strategist is deciding whether to keep a promise to Sophie while pursuing time with Tabitha.

The collector keeps a photograph or invitation.

The comedy player posts something that causes the event to be interpreted as political.

All of those pleasures remain subordinate to the same authored narrative event.

# Three applied scenario solutions

## 1. The community hall

### Current abstraction risk

The player learns the hall is threatened, talks to people, chooses whether to help and later receives state changes.

### Narrative-support version

The player already has a routine there.

One evening:

- the usual door is locked;
- the normal group is outside;
- a new notice contradicts the earlier email;
- equipment is visible through the window;
- Maya is trying not to appear upset.

The player can:

- approach Maya;
- photograph the notice;
- wait for the caretaker;
- accompany somebody to another entrance;
- leave;
- promise limited help;
- publicly post the contradiction;
- say they cannot take this on.

If they become involved, the main convergence is not a venue-management puzzle. It might be:

- a meeting;
- a replacement radio night;
- a public consultation;
- a final ordinary evening before closure.

Inside the event, the player chooses:

- who to sit with;
- whether to speak;
- whether to show private evidence;
- whether to support Maya publicly;
- whether to leave when another commitment begins.

Afterward:

- the place changes;
- the group chat changes;
- an object or photograph remains;
- routines relocate or continue;
- people remember the player’s conduct.

The hall matters because the player lived there before, acted there during the crisis and experiences its aftermath.

---

## 2. A first date

### Current abstraction risk

Choose destination, read dialogue, choose romantic responses, receive relationship change.

### Narrative-support version

Beforehand:

- the player chooses whether to make a concrete plan or leave it vague;
- chooses what to wear from existing meaningful outfits;
- can bring, return or deliberately keep something associated with the person;
- may arrive early, on time or late because of another situation.

During the date:

- conversation continues while walking;
- the route exposes social context;
- somebody calls;
- the player may answer, decline or put the phone away;
- the date may want to enter somewhere the player dislikes;
- they choose whether to sit beside or opposite;
- silence is permitted;
- leaving together or separately is physically enacted.

Afterward:

- a ticket, photograph, borrowed item or message remains;
- the journey home provides afterglow;
- the next contact reflects what actually happened;
- the location may become part of their shared life or somewhere neither wants to revisit.

No romance score is necessary.

---

## 3. Workplace restructuring

### Current abstraction risk

NPCs discuss restructuring while the hidden network changes jobs and moods.

### Narrative-support version

The player experiences:

- somebody’s empty desk;
- a changed seating plan;
- a printer being removed;
- a management email using reassuring language;
- colleagues speaking differently depending on who is present;
- a formerly private room being used by consultants.

The player can:

- ask;
- cover for an absent person;
- forward or retain an email;
- sit with a particular group at lunch;
- accompany somebody to a meeting;
- make a public joke;
- behave as though nothing is happening;
- exploit a resulting opening.

Consequences appear through:

- changed routines;
- access to rooms;
- who confides in the player;
- work assignments;
- social invitations;
- who remains in the building;
- an object left behind;
- later references to what the player did when everyone was uncertain.

The world situation remains authored and simulated. The player is not solving restructuring. They are **living inside it**.

# What should now be killed, demoted or retained

## Kill

### “The toy must work without narrative”

Replace it with:

> **The interaction must produce identifiable narrative value and remain low-friction enough not to compete with the story.**

### Physical logistics as the main execution phase

No event-layout optimisation, equipment routing or crew configuration as the central game.

These could appear occasionally when narratively relevant, but not as the core.

### A universal social/common-sense ontology

Do not attempt to support every plausible human action through generic semantic combination.

Use:

- a small recurring narrative grammar;
- context-specific actions;
- bespoke landmark interactions.

### Tangible rewards as conventional loot

Do not force equipment, upgrades or collections into the design merely to resemble an RPG.

---

## Demote

### Life Schemes

Retain it as a lightweight internal model of:

- what the player is currently attempting;
- what they have promised;
- what facts and relationships are nearby.

Do not make it a player-facing plan-construction game.

### Desire Ecology

Retain:

- attractive previews;
- low-cost sampling;
- one foreground pursuit;
- Open Life intervals;
- aftermath.

Demote the large runtime hook-generation machinery until the underlying scenes and interactions have demonstrated that players actually form desires.

### Convergence events

Keep them, but redefine them as:

> **authored social and emotional set pieces whose live spatial, conversational and material state reflects the player’s history.**

Do not treat them as nonviolent boss fights requiring a broad action toolbox.

### Affordance progression

Keep it, but express progression through:

- social permission;
- habitual use;
- material traces;
- new contexts;
- physical changes;
- remembered conduct.

Not only hidden access states.

---

## Retain strongly

- authored headline characters;
- relationships and romance;
- British institutional satire;
- physical dioramas;
- the phone;
- diegetic time and routine compression;
- hidden situations expressed through multiple channels;
- narrative attention and pacing;
- persistent consequences;
- landmark and connective content;
- warm ordinary life;
- the player’s ability to be kind, selfish, awkward, dishonest, reckless or ridiculous.

The next prototype must therefore test the corrected hypothesis directly.

## Build a Narrative Interaction Lab

Create three small, strongly written slices.

### Slice A — Intimacy

A date, late-night conversation or relationship rupture.

Tests:

- silence;
- movement during conversation;
- physical gesture;
- meaningful object;
- afterglow.

### Slice B — Ensemble social scene

A small party, radio night, workplace gathering or family meal.

Tests:

- presence;
- spatial social choice;
- multiple conversations;
- information flow;
- public versus private conduct.

### Slice C — Institutional situation

A council office, work restructure, housing dispute or public consultation.

Tests:

- environmental evidence;
- conflicting accounts;
- phone/world integration;
- provisional interpretation;
- public trace.

Each slice should contain final-quality-enough writing to test character and emotional effect. A narrative-first game cannot be validated using completely disposable placeholder prose. The scope must be small rather than the narrative quality being absent.

---

## Prototype each slice in three forms

| Version | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Dialogue baseline** | Establish what the authored narrative achieves alone |
| **Narrative-support version** | Add presence, expression, material action and residue |
| **Mechanically stronger version** | Test whether added challenge/system depth helps or dilutes |

Do not tell testers which is preferred.

---

## Success evidence

Continue with the narrative-support grammar when players:

- retell scenes using first-person actions rather than only summarising dialogue;
- mention where they stood, what they kept, whom they followed or what they withheld;
- correctly understand character motives without excessive exposition;
- believe their conduct changed the meaning of the scene;
- recognise consequences without numerical UI;
- form different impressions and priorities across playthroughs;
- want to see the aftermath;
- remember physical places and objects alongside the characters;
- extract different pleasures from the same scene;
- do not describe the interactions as chores or minigames.

A particularly strong result would sound like:

> “I didn’t tell her. I sat with her until the call came, then I answered it, and I think that’s why she left without me.”

That is narrative interaction.

---

## Kill or redesign evidence

Remove or simplify a mechanism when:

- players focus on performing it correctly rather than on the scene;
- an optimal strategy overrides role-playing;
- the interaction delays a moment without changing its meaning;
- testers cannot explain why the action was available;
- a bespoke mechanic needs substantial tutorialisation;
- the same interaction becomes repetitive after several uses;
- the mechanic is remembered more strongly than the character for the wrong reason;
- objects feel like inventory loot;
- the physical world remains a corridor between dialogue triggers;
- players cannot tell what changed;
- the support version performs worse than the dialogue baseline.

# Recommended final architecture

```text
AUTHORED CHARACTERS, SITUATIONS AND DRAMATIC MATERIAL
                         ↓
              DIEGETIC WORLD PRESENTATION
       place / routine / phone / objects / other people
                         ↓
             PLAYER NARRATIVE PARTICIPATION
      presence / expression / interpretation / commitment /
               material gesture / withdrawal
                         ↓
          CHARACTER AND WORLD INTERPRETATION
                         ↓
          IMMEDIATE SOCIAL OR PHYSICAL RESPONSE
                         ↓
                  PERSISTENT RESIDUE
      relationship behaviour / object / place / message /
         routine / access / public account / memory
                         ↓
              LATER AUTHORED REINCORPORATION
```

The world simulation determines what is true.

The authored narrative determines what is dramatically strong.

The player determines how they participate.

The support mechanics make that participation concrete.

The narrative layer regulates attention and reincorporates the resulting history.

# Final design position

The professional-quality direction is not to find *Managed Decline’s* equivalent of combat.

It is to build:

> ## **A consistent narrative interaction language, supplemented by carefully chosen landmark micro-mechanics and a persistent material record of the player’s life.**

The canonical recurring grammar should be:

> **Be there—or don’t.  
> Notice.  
> Interpret.  
> Speak, act or withhold.  
> Commit or withdraw.  
> Leave something behind.  
> Return to a world that remembers.**

This supports the game’s core values rather than diluting them.

The story remains the reason to play.

The characters remain the main rewards.

The diorama makes the story spatial.

The phone makes it continuous.

Time makes choices consequential.

Objects and rituals make relationships material.

Residue makes the player’s life visible.

Different players obtain character attachment, role-playing, discovery, aesthetic inhabitation, light strategy, collection, comedy or replay variation from the same authored social world.

The immediate production priority should therefore be the three-slice Narrative Interaction Lab—not another global narrative architecture layer, and not a general-purpose mechanical sandbox.

# Conclusions by prototype

## 1. The Borrowed Coat: do not operationalise subtext

The dialogue baseline scored `4` for presence, comprehension, agency and pull, with burden `2`. You remembered the rain, bus stop and Tabitha looking down the road. You understood staying and declining Sophie’s call as signs of interest, even though the game did not convert them into visible romance values.

The support version instead asked you to:

- sit beside her;
- start removing the coat;
- not move away from her touch;
- turn the phone over;
- walk her to the door.

Those are individually plausible actions, but together they formed an obvious romance sequence. You described them as the only sensible actions if your intention was to sleep with her. Presence, comprehension and agency all fell to `2`, while burden rose to `4`.

The important lesson is:

> **Do not split a coherent emotional intention into several mandatory gestures.**

The baseline choice:

> “Do you actually want it back?”

is dramatically dense. It can mean:

- Are you using the coat as an excuse?
- Do you want to see me again?
- Am I welcome to continue this?
- Will you admit what you want?
- Can I signal interest without exposing myself completely?

By contrast:

> Sit beside her

contains much less interpretive material.

The game can still **animate** the protagonist sitting closer, adjusting the coat, declining the call and accompanying her to the bus. Those actions do not all need separate player inputs. The player chose the conduct through the dialogue; staging can realise it physically.

### Decision

For intimate scenes:

- dialogue and silence should carry most agency;
- physical gestures should usually be automatic realisations of selected intent;
- expose a separate material action only when it creates a genuinely distinct meaning or lasting consequence.

For example:

> Return the coat now  
> Keep it until next time  
> Ask her to collect it from your flat

That decision changes the relationship object and future situation. It earns an explicit input. Clicking through every intermediate gesture does not.

The support mechanisms therefore had mixed value.

### What probably helped

- deciding whom to attend to first;
- deciding whether Sophie enters with you;
- connecting two characters;
- deciding whether evidence is private or public;
- physically locating the player within an ensemble.

These choices alter the **social configuration** of the scene.

### What probably hurt

- exposing every transition as a separate command;
- presenting the event as a sequence of operations;
- making the action model more salient than the characters;
- moving attention from “What is happening here?” to “What outcome does this action produce?”

### Better implementation

The map might let the player decide:

> Wait by the door for Sophie  
> Go to Maya backstage  
> Join Theo near the equipment

That determines the scene’s entry state.

Then the game returns to dialogue-baseline interaction.

Later, one decisive choice may combine action and intention:

> Give Maya the photograph privately  
> Show it to the room  
> Post it yourself  
> Keep it out of this

There is no need for a separate state sidebar, evidence inventory or intermediate **Place phone beside script** command unless that exact gesture has dramatic importance.

---

## 3. Temporary Adjustment: optional investigation is promising, but it creates agency expectations

The institutional baseline again scored `4` for presence, comprehension, agency and pull. You specifically enjoyed figuring out the situation rather than being told what it meant. The weakness was choice construction: only one option usually seemed prudent, while the others felt reckless or self-destructive. You still felt control because one option represented what you wanted to do, but you correctly identified that several credible alternatives would produce stronger authorship.

The support version also remained immersive:

- presence `4`;
- comprehension `4`;
- pull `4`;
- burden `2`;
- agency `3`.

Reading the notice and lifting it to find the crossed-out timetable were sensible physical interactions because they revealed **new narrative evidence**. The failure occurred at the end: the available actions did not include what you wanted to do. You wanted to intervene yourself rather than return the decision to Maya.

This identifies a serious design effect:

## **Affordance debt**

Once the game teaches the player:

> You can inspect the environment, gather evidence and act physically,

the player reasonably expects to be able to formulate an action from what they learned.

If the resulting action space remains as narrow as a conventional dialogue tree, the added support system can make the game feel **more restrictive**, not less. The game has raised the promise of agency without funding it.

The baseline avoids some of this because its contract is clearer:

> You are choosing among authored interpretations and responses.

The support version implies:

> You are operating within a broader world of actions.

That contract is much more expensive to fulfil.

### Decision

Optional environmental investigation is worth retaining, but it should:

1. reveal information;
2. modify later dialogue;
3. produce immediate character response;
4. not imply unrestricted problem-solving;
5. culminate in several plausible authored actions.

The player might inspect the notice, then receive choices such as:

- ask Maya to wait while you call somebody;
- post a cautious public question;
- show the contradiction privately to another character;
- tell Maya you think the hall is closing;
- advise her not to assume anything yet;
- leave because this is not yours to take over.

The environment supplies context. Dialogue remains the main surface for deciding what that context means.

---

# What the system-forward test established

The system-forward versions should be abandoned as a player-facing direction.

That does **not** mean Managed Decline cannot use hidden numerical or categorical state internally. It means the game should not present relationships, events or interpretation as an optimisation interface.

The failure pattern was consistent:

- an announced goal displaced personal interpretation;
- visible values defined what “good play” meant;
- dialogue became flavour text around calculations;
- gestures became resource conversions;
- ambiguity became something to eliminate;
- the player began clicking for outcomes rather than attending to people.

In the ensemble test, the goal announcement caused you to optimise Maya’s support values, and you explicitly stopped reading.

In the intimacy test, the system made consequences clear but felt like “completing a tax form.”

The proper design rule is:

> **Clarity should come from character behaviour, scene framing and visible consequences—not from showing the internal evaluation model.**

A hidden relationship model can still determine:

- what Tabitha assumes;
- what Maya remembers;
- whether somebody trusts the player;
- what opportunities later become plausible.

Its output should be:

- a changed greeting;
- a message;
- an invitation;
- silence;
- a person choosing to tell the player something;
- altered access;
- a visible action.

Not:

> `Maya +2`

# Why the dialogue baseline felt richest

The baseline benefited from five things.

## 1. High semantic bandwidth

Each choice could determine several dimensions at once:

- what the protagonist believes;
- what they want;
- how directly they express it;
- what social risk they accept;
- what information they reveal;
- what tone they use.

A physical micro-action often determines only one.

## 2. Controlled compression

The writer could move directly between dramatically important moments.

The support version had to stop and ask the player to enact transitions that the baseline could communicate fluently through prose, animation or implication.

## 3. Preserved subtext

The game did not explain the emotional function of each gesture.

The player could infer:

> Staying means something.

That inference is part of the pleasure.

## 4. Fewer attention switches

There was no need to alternate between:

- reading characters;
- reading state panels;
- interpreting a world diagram;
- selecting a movement;
- checking evidence;
- calculating consequences.

Attention remained on the human situation.

## 5. Imagination completed the scene

You remembered rain, the bus stop and Tabitha looking down the road even though these were not interactive systems.

The physical world was already contributing. It did so through **framing and imagery**, not manipulation.

---

# The unresolved issue: can dialogue-baseline play sustain a full game?

The test does **not** establish that four-option dialogue scenes can sustain twenty or thirty hours.

It establishes something narrower:

> Adding more control surfaces did not improve these scenes and often damaged them.

The sustainability problem remains real. But the answer should not be “put more mechanics inside every conversation.”

The baseline can sustain a longer game only if the **larger experience changes around it**.

## Variation must come from scene structure

Managed Decline should alternate among:

- focused VN conversations;
- live walk-and-talk scenes;
- ensemble events;
- ambient overheard dialogue;
- phone calls;
- text and group-chat exchanges;
- silent or nearly silent aftermath;
- work scenes;
- travel;
- montage;
- exploration intervals;
- interruptions;
- scenes where no choice is required;
- scenes with one decisive choice;
- scenes containing several competing plausible choices.

The player should not experience:

> dialogue panel → four options → dialogue panel → four options

at an unchanging cadence for hours.

## The decision space must evolve

Early relationship choices may concern:

- whether to approach;
- curiosity;
- first impressions;
- access.

Later choices should concern:

- trust;
- conflicting loyalties;
- public versus private support;
- shared commitments;
- dependency;
- future plans;
- unresolved history.

The UI can remain simple while the **meaning of the decisions deepens**.

## Player agency should exist around scenes as well as inside them

The player can decide:

- where to go;
- whom to contact;
- whether to attend;
- who to approach first;
- whether to stay;
- whom to accompany;
- what information to bring;
- whether to answer an interruption;
- when to leave.

This makes the campaign more than a linear sequence of dialogue trees without requiring mechanical minigames.

## Consequences must reincorporate

Dialogue is sustainable when later scenes remember:

- what the player said;
- what they withheld;
- where they were;
- who witnessed it;
- what they promised;
- whom they prioritised;
- whether they returned;
- what object or photograph remains.

Without reincorporation, even excellent dialogue eventually feels disposable.

---

# The point of the diorama and map

The diorama does not need to justify itself as a mechanical playground.

Its role should be:

> ## **The persistent physical interface to the narrative world.**

A useful division of responsibility is:

| Layer | Primary job |
|---|---|
| **Diorama/map** | Notice, locate, approach, avoid, sequence, witness |
| **Dialogue** | Interpret, express, negotiate, commit |
| **Phone** | Remember, communicate, anticipate, compare accounts |
| **Time/routine** | Create expectation, opportunity cost and rhythm |
| **World simulation** | Continue events and determine reactions |
| **Persistent environment** | Display aftermath and accumulated history |

## Specifically, the map provides:

### Social geography

The player sees:

- who is together;
- who is alone;
- who has arrived;
- who left;
- who is somewhere unusual;
- who appears welcome or out of place.

### Voluntary sequencing

The player chooses:

- Maya first or Sophie first;
- pub or hall;
- go home or investigate;
- approach the argument or keep walking.

The story does not need to ask these through an abstract menu.

### Anticipation

Seeing Tabitha at the station, an empty desk at work or police outside the hall creates a question before any dialogue begins.

### Pacing

Walking through a small attractive space, hearing music and ambient conversation, or taking a bus home provides decompression between dense scenes.

### Place attachment

The same café, station, flat and hall acquire emotional meaning through repeated use.

### Visible consequence

The player returns and sees:

- the notice has changed;
- someone’s desk is gone;
- the hall is open;
- two people now sit together;
- a shop has closed;
- a poster remains;
- a photograph is displayed;
- a routine has moved elsewhere.

### Discovery without formal objectives

The player may notice a situation because it is physically happening, not because a quest has been announced.

### British identity and aesthetic pleasure

The diorama is itself part of the product promise. It gives the game a place-specific visual, musical and cultural identity.

That is enough to justify it—provided the maps are compact, dense and frequently changed. The player should not walk for minutes through empty decorative space.

---

# The optimal support level: **dialogue-first with diegetic punctuation**

The current best hypothesis is not pure dialogue and not the tested support model.

It is:

> ## **Dialogue baseline + passive physical staging + map-level agency + sparse optional intervention.**

## Layer 1 — Passive support: always present

These require no additional player input:

- character positioning;
- facial and body animation;
- characters entering and leaving;
- objects in their hands or homes;
- environmental changes;
- weather;
- ambient NPC activity;
- music and sound;
- automatic physical realisation of chosen dialogue intent.

This should provide most of the embodiment.

## Layer 2 — Map-level agency: frequent

Use the diorama for:

- where to go;
- whom to approach;
- whether to avoid;
- whom to follow;
- whether to stay;
- who receives the player’s attention first.

This is high-value because it changes the **context of the next authored scene** without interrupting that scene internally.

## Layer 3 — Optional observation: occasional

Examples:

- read a notice;
- inspect an object;
- listen at the edge of a group;
- look through a window;
- check a message;
- photograph something;
- compare two visible accounts.

These actions should reveal material that changes subsequent dialogue.

They should not populate an exposed evidence meter.

## Layer 4 — Decisive material actions: sparse

Examples:

- give or keep the coat;
- post or delete the photograph;
- hand Maya the evidence privately;
- sign or refuse a document;
- return a key;
- answer or decline the call;
- leave with one person rather than another.

These are worth explicit input because they:

- have social meaning;
- create residue;
- change witnesses or information flow;
- cannot be reduced to cosmetic animation.

## Layer 5 — Landmark micro-mechanics: rare

A signature scenario may contain one bespoke interaction whose form embodies its theme—for example navigating Tabitha’s old institutional training programme.

These should not become the recurring interaction grammar.

---

# Support-mechanism requirements

A support action should be exposed only when it does something that the dialogue baseline cannot do as well.

It must change at least one of:

- **presence** — where the player is or who they are with;
- **witnesses** — who sees or hears something;
- **knowledge** — what the player can reasonably infer;
- **information flow** — who receives evidence or a claim;
- **commitment** — what somebody now expects;
- **material residue** — what remains in the world;
- **timing** — whether the player stays, leaves, waits or interrupts.

It should be removed or automated when it merely:

- confirms the already selected emotional direction;
- performs an obvious next gesture;
- adds another click between authored beats;
- converts subtext into a visible procedure;
- exposes internal state;
- implies a wider action space the game cannot support.

## Starting interaction budget

This is a prototype constraint, not a final numerical law:

- most focused dialogue scenes should contain **zero** separate support actions;
- scenes with a real abstraction gap may contain **one**;
- investigative or ensemble scenes may occasionally contain **two**;
- never chain several support actions without an immediate character or world response.

The Borrowed Coat support version had five explicit steps. Most should have been staging.

---

# The next design problem is choice quality

The baseline’s principal weakness was not insufficient mechanics. It was that some decisions had only one credible option.

For major choices, aim for at least two options that a reasonable player could genuinely want.

The distinction should not be:

- sensible;
- reckless;
- obviously cruel.

It should be something like:

- cautious but possibly passive;
- public but likely to damage trust;
- private but potentially ineffective;
- personally loyal but institutionally compromising;
- honest but socially costly;
- ambiguous but preserves options.

The player should sometimes think:

> “I could genuinely do either of these.”

That is stronger agency than adding another input mode.

Not every choice needs equal difficulty. Relaxed, expressive and obvious choices also have value. But repeated false choices will eventually make the baseline feel like reading a story with occasional confirmation buttons.

# Recommended next prototype

The next prototype should test **session sustainability and support dosage**, not repeat the baseline/support/system comparison.

## A 25–40 minute “one evening” slice

Include:

- one compact diorama;
- three accessible locations;
- five or six characters;
- one intimate scene;
- one ensemble scene;
- one institutional or public contradiction;
- one phone interruption;
- one visible aftermath;
- no announced objective;
- no meters;
- no state sidebar.

Compare:

### Version A — Dialogue baseline with passive diorama

The player chooses destinations and characters, but substantial scenes use baseline dialogue.

### Version B — Baseline plus optional observation

Add one or two genuinely optional environmental discoveries that modify later dialogue.

### Version C — Baseline plus sparse decisive action

Add only one material or spatial intervention to each major scene.

The test should be blind and counterbalanced across several testers.

The main questions are:

- Does dialogue fatigue appear over a full session?
- Does the map generate curiosity without becoming dead travel time?
- Do players make use of optional observation?
- Does one decisive physical action improve memory and ownership?
- At what point does support begin to feel like process?
- Do players remember places and movements as well as dialogue?
- Do they spontaneously form intentions before the game declares them?
- Are there multiple plausible choices at the important turns?

# Final design position

The first prototype did not show that the map and support systems are unnecessary.

It showed where they belong.

> **The map should carry context, discovery, sequence and aftermath.  
> Dialogue should carry interpretation, expression and commitment.  
> Sparse diegetic actions should punctuate decisions that genuinely need physical form.  
> Hidden systems should make the world remember without exposing the calculation.**

The baseline felt richest because it maintained dramatic continuity and let each input carry considerable meaning.

The optimal direction is therefore not “pure VN” and not “more gameplay.”

It is:

> ## **A dialogue-first social RPG in which the diorama makes the narrative spatial, voluntary and persistent—but usually does not interrupt a strong scene merely to prove that the environment is interactive.**

The next risk to solve is **long-form rhythm and choice quality**, not lack of mechanics.
