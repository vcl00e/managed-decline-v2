# Policy-change adventure: companion agency and learning

**Date:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Provisional design direction / findings to preserve, **not a lock on character design or spatial mechanics**

## Context

We explored how a Managed Decline-style game could turn the real complexity of changing UK government policy into something readable, entertaining, educational, and narratively rich. The discussion then focused on the player experience when Tabitha, Gary, Binface, or other major companions already know what they are doing, discover parts of the process themselves, and interact with one another.

The important outcome is **not** a final character schema or map/navigation design. Those remain open and should continue to be discovered through research, narrative work, and prototypes.

## Durable findings

### 1. Simplify the player's decision surface, not the causal system

The political system underneath the story can remain complicated, but the player should normally face only a few understandable live possibilities at once.

The game should compress procedure, repetition, and administrative detail while preserving causal truths such as:

- different institutions possess different kinds of authority;
- evidence, public legitimacy, political sponsorship, money, implementation capacity, and timing are not interchangeable;
- attention is not the same as authority;
- passing a law is not the same as delivering an outcome;
- institutions contain people with conflicting beliefs and incentives;
- apparent victories can create new problems or implementation failures.

The player should understand these relationships because they experience them, not because they are shown a civics diagram or a large political dashboard.

### 2. Introduce institutions only when a concrete obstacle makes them relevant

Do not require the player to learn the whole machinery of British government in advance.

A new institution, process, or political concept should normally enter the player's mental model because someone poses a concrete problem such as:

- "Can you prove this?"
- "Who will pay for it?"
- "Who actually has the power to approve it?"
- "Will councils / hospitals / businesses be able to deliver it?"
- "Why would a minister spend political capital on this now?"

This lets the heroes and player gradually discover how change works together.

### 3. Major companions must be competent protagonists, not followers waiting for player instructions

Tabitha, Gary, Binface, and any comparable major characters should know things the player does not, notice opportunities independently, and sometimes solve problems without the player.

Avoid the common RPG failure where the player becomes the only intelligent or effective person in the group.

A companion should sometimes be able to say, in effect, "I know this part; trust me," and then demonstrate real competence.

Likewise, the characters should sometimes discover an important route, person, fact, or political mechanism before the player does.

### 4. Competent companions must not steal player agency

The solution is not to make companions ignorant. Player agency can instead come from **judgment under competing valid possibilities**.

Different companions can perceive different opportunities, risks, or priorities. The player may decide:

- whose judgment to back;
- who to accompany;
- what to prioritise under time pressure;
- whether to reconcile competing plans;
- what compromises are acceptable;
- when to trust a companion's expertise;
- when to challenge them because they are overlooking something.

The player therefore need not be the greatest economist, organiser, communicator, or policy expert in the party. Their role can emerge from travelling with capable people and making consequential judgments among incomplete perspectives.

### 5. No major character should have a complete theory of change

A useful general principle is that major characters can be **strong in different ways and predictably incomplete in different ways**.

This prevents both flat tutorial characters and contrived stupidity.

For discussion we explored a possible split such as:

- one character being unusually sensitive to people, lived experience, trust, and organising;
- another being unusually strong at systems, diagnosis, economics, evidence, or causal explanation;
- another being unusually strong at public attention, accessibility, spectacle, ridicule, or democratic participation.

**This split is explicitly not locked down.** It is an example of the broader principle that companions should have real, differentiated competence and blind spots. Their authentic real-world/parody territory still needs to determine their final designs.

### 6. Companion disagreement should arise from substantive views, not manufactured party banter

Conflicts between major characters should often be conflicts between useful but incomplete interpretations of the same problem:

- lived reality versus abstraction;
- urgency versus evidence;
- attention versus credibility;
- moral purity versus coalition breadth;
- national ambition versus local feasibility;
- ideal policy versus implementable policy.

Both sides should sometimes be right.

A decision should therefore not usually resolve into "correct companion / wrong companion." Choosing one route should create a different next problem, missing piece, or opportunity.

### 7. Characters should learn from one another while remaining recognisable

A strong long-term arc is for companions to acquire small pieces of each other's judgment without converging into the same personality.

The player should notice this behaviourally rather than through explicit growth meters.

Examples of the *type* of change (not locked character beats):

- a systems-focused character begins asking who has actually experienced the problem;
- a community-focused character becomes sharper about who possesses formal authority;
- a publicity-focused character becomes more careful about when spectacle helps or exploits people;
- a sceptical character learns that another companion's apparently ridiculous method is occasionally extremely effective.

Character development and political education can therefore become the same process.

### 8. Companions should initiate actions and plans

Major companions should have intentions independent of the player.

They may:

- propose a route;
- start walking somewhere;
- approach someone they recognise;
- notice evidence or an inconsistency;
- arrange a meeting;
- pursue a task while the player is elsewhere;
- return with information or a partial victory;
- object when the player ignores something they regard as important.

If a companion makes a meaningful suggestion and the player ignores it, the world should not behave as though the suggestion never happened. The companion can react, adapt, pursue another route, remember the disagreement, or raise it later.

### 9. Companion-to-companion relationships are first-class narrative content

Do not make every important relationship orbit the player.

Major companions should:

- talk directly to one another;
- remember events the player did not witness;
- develop their own trust, irritation, jokes, admiration, and disagreements;
- defend or criticise one another;
- sometimes reach decisions before the player arrives;
- change one another over time.

This is necessary if the group is supposed to feel like people undertaking an adventure together rather than NPC spokes attached to the player avatar.

### 10. The world should continue when the player is not looking

The party may sometimes split because several opportunities exist at once.

The player can choose which thread to personally experience while the others continue acting.

When they regroup, the other characters should have outcomes, discoveries, failures, stories, and perhaps new complications of their own.

This makes the political world feel larger than the player's camera and avoids implying that nothing can happen without player intervention.

### 11. Failure should normally change the route rather than produce a dead end

Political attempts can fail without becoming conventional game-over conditions.

A refusal, failed vote, poor pilot, hostile article, or lost ally can create:

- a different coalition;
- a media route;
- a constituency route;
- an evidence route;
- a later political window;
- a redesigned policy;
- an unexpected character conflict or growth beat.

This is both more faithful to real political change and more narratively useful.

### 12. Feedback should be visible in people and the world wherever possible

Avoid flattening political change into exposed numerical meters if diegetic feedback can communicate the same information.

Examples:

- someone who ignored the group now approaches them;
- a previously closed door becomes accessible;
- attendance at a meeting changes;
- characters begin repeating the campaign's framing;
- a journalist's tone changes;
- an institution asks a different kind of question;
- an implementation site physically or socially changes;
- companions respond immediately to the player's priorities or omissions.

Important actions can have immediate feedback while their larger policy consequences remain delayed and uncertain.

## Player-experience hypothesis worth testing

A promising player fantasy is **not**:

> I am the genius who masters British government and tells everyone what to do.

Nor:

> I am a passive observer following competent heroes.

A stronger possibility is:

> I am travelling with ambitious, capable, imperfect people. They know things I do not. I know or notice things they miss. We disagree, learn, divide work, make compromises, surprise one another, and gradually become better at changing things together.

The player can become important partly because they connect people, perspectives, opportunities, and judgments rather than because all expertise originates with them.

This is a hypothesis to prototype, not a fixed player-role definition.

## Spatial / navigation implications discussed but deliberately **not committed**

The discussion generated several potentially useful spatial ideas, including:

- letting political access become physically visible through doors, meetings, and changing routes;
- introducing institutional spaces only when the narrative creates a reason to visit them;
- allowing companions to lead, walk beside the player, split off, or propose destinations;
- treating navigation choices as decisions about which opportunity or companion to follow;
- using places to embody different kinds of political constraint;
- returning to affected communities or implementation sites so policy consequences become visible.

Earlier we also explored possible abstractions such as functional space families (community/evidence/public/power/delivery), hub-and-network maps, compressed Westminster, and a limited set of live opportunities.

**None of these spatial structures are accepted as the final design.** In particular, do not infer from this note that Managed Decline should use a hub map, five space categories, a particular navigation UI, a companion-follow system, or any specific overworld topology. These remain prototype questions.

## Character implications discussed but deliberately **not committed**

The conversation used Tabitha, Gary, and Binface to test how differentiated competence could work. The exact division of expertise, habits, flaws, relationships, growth arcs, theories of change, comic functions, and leadership roles remains open.

Do not lock in:

- "Tabitha = people";
- "Gary = system";
- "Binface = attention";
- any particular pairwise conflict;
- any particular policy expertise;
- any specific companion mechanic derived from those labels.

What **is** worth retaining is the design requirement that the headline characters remain unmistakable, autonomous, competent, lovable protagonists with their own authentic ambitions and perspectives, rather than becoming interchangeable tutorial devices.

## Prototyping questions

Future prototypes should test, rather than assume:

1. Can a companion demonstrate competence without making the player feel unnecessary?
2. Can two or more companions propose genuinely reasonable but conflicting next moves?
3. Does choosing whom to accompany feel like authorship of the journey rather than selecting content from a menu?
4. Can companions make progress off-screen without making the player feel robbed of important scenes?
5. Do companion-to-companion interactions make the group feel autonomous and alive?
6. When the player ignores a companion's suggestion, does the reaction feel natural and consequential rather than punitive?
7. Can the game teach an institutional concept through a concrete obstacle with little or no explicit tutorialisation?
8. Can the player explain afterwards *why* a political outcome happened without seeing underlying simulation numbers?
9. Can failure open a compelling route rather than feeling like a missed success state?
10. Can visible world/relationship feedback communicate political progress clearly enough without dashboardification?

## Current conclusion

Preserve the general direction:

> **Complex political causality underneath; simple immediate choices on the surface; autonomous competent companions; player judgment rather than player omniscience; political education emerging through shared experience, disagreement, discovery, and consequence.**

Keep the precise character models and spatial/navigation mechanics open until prototypes demonstrate which implementations actually create the desired experience.
