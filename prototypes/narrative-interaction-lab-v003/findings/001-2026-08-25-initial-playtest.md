## Route taken

1. joined Tabitha's joke;
2. asked why the old programme mattered to her;
3. watched Tabitha instead of the projector;
4. flirted during the paired role-play;
5. checked whether the fabricated quote was real;
6. offered Tabitha an exit;
7. identified that the programme kept the insult while cutting her argument;
8. let Tabitha decide what to do when her phone exposed the situation;
9. chose not to turn her into a public spectacle;
10. left with her;
11. returned to the opening Option D flirt.

The hidden relationship state progressed through `amused → curious → seen → openly_flirty → attentive → safe → understood → trusted → safe_person`.

This is useful evidence that expressive dialogue can describe a coherent relationship through conduct rather than through an exposed relationship meter.

## What clearly worked

### 1. The prototype earned continuation

Unlike v002, the participant wanted to continue immediately and remained entertained through the full slice. The short-form hook/comedy/character-first correction worked for this tester.

### 2. Tabitha became rewarding company

The strongest qualitative result was not merely that the premise was interesting; the participant valued spending time with Tabitha herself. The character therefore functioned as an ongoing reward rather than only as an initial hook.

### 3. The emotional journey landed

The transition from shared comedy and flirtatious complicity into the fabricated redemption quote, private explanation, exposure and final choice produced clear emotional movement rather than a flat causal sequence.

### 4. The final choice supported nuanced satire

The tester concluded that there were no simple bad actors in the room and therefore did not want to make anyone's life difficult. They chose to leave with Tabitha rather than punish Gareth or turn Tabitha into spectacle.

This supports a useful Managed Decline satirical territory: absurdity can emerge from institutions, incentives, simplification and ordinary people trying to function inside them without requiring every scenario to contain a villain.

## Material weaknesses

### 1. Expressive-choice feedback was too weak

The tester reported that they often could not understand the effect of their choices, sometimes feeling as though there was no feedback.

The clearest example was `Watch Tabitha instead of the projector.` Internally this set `watchedTabitha`, changed the relationship state to `seen`, and stored a memory, but the fiction did not sufficiently show that Tabitha noticed or interpreted the gesture.

Accepted correction:

> **Expressive choices do not need large branches or numerical rewards, but important expressions should usually receive perceptible fictional acknowledgement.**

A small look, gesture, changed line, callback or later memory can close the loop:

`I express something → the character perceives it → I perceive that they perceived it.`

### 2. The scenario's inciting causality is prototype-grade

The tester could not make convincing sense of why Tabitha had chosen to attend the workshop in the first place. Later discussion clarified that the problem was **not** that her motives were initially opaque. Figuring people out can be desirable.

The issue was doubt about whether a coherent explanation existed underneath the opacity.

On review, the existing chain — notice of a “legacy learning session,” wondering whether she was “the legacy,” and wanting to see whether they had “fixed the ending” — is too arbitrary and weak to constitute production-quality causal engineering.

The participant did **not** consider bringing the protagonist itself implausible. If Tabitha and the protagonist are close, bringing a trusted person for support is natural and need not be explicitly explained.

Accepted principle:

> **The player may be uncertain. The author must not be. Opacity is allowed; arbitrary behaviour is not.**

For important character-driven situations, the author/system should have a concrete underlying account of why the situation is happening, why the character is there, what they know, what they expect, what they are unsure about, and what they would likely do without the player. The player may reconstruct this gradually or never receive it as exposition.

However, v003 does **not** need its inciting lore repaired merely to make the prototype production-ready. The Community Compass premise is test material, not accepted scenario canon. Richer established character/world lore should make future scenarios arise more naturally rather than requiring backwards justification from a funny set piece.

## Comedy interpretation

The comedy rating was 5/5, but the written debrief described the scenario as “a bit funny.” Therefore the supported conclusion is that comedy crossed the threshold needed to sustain the scene and make Tabitha enjoyable company — **not** that Managed Decline's comedy problem is solved.

## Evidence-level conclusion

For this tester, v003 strongly supports the following experiential sequence:

`compelling human/satirical premise → desire to spend time with a character → comedy/complicity → authored emotional turn → meaningful participation → relationship payoff → desire for another scene`

It also supports retaining arrangement/social state as hidden infrastructure beneath that experience rather than making the infrastructure itself the content.
